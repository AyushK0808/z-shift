\begin{abstract}
DUSt3R (Dense Unconstrained Stereo 3D Reconstruction) and MASt3R (Matching and
Stereo 3D Reconstruction) recover dense scene geometry from 2D observations
alone, without camera pose or prior calibration. A deployable 2D-to-3D system,
however, requires ingestion, frame selection, temporal synchronization,
geometry refinement, and application-specific delivery to work together. We
present z-shift, a four-stage pipeline in which each stage is replaceable, and
data passes between stages only as serializable artifacts. Ingestion unifies
images, videos, synchronized captures, and live feeds under a common spatial
schema. Reconstruction uses MASt3R for the initial scene geometry, may
optionally fuse Truncated Signed Distance Functions (TSDFs), and falls back to
a point map when fusion is unavailable. Refinement applies a fixed sequence of
operations, while delivery routes GLB or point-cloud output under a
compatibility contract. Our evaluation combines a constrained benchmark suite
with one eight-view capture in which each stage was traced. Without preserving
capture order, motion-ranked frame retention degrades sliding-window pairing
locality 6.6-fold, while restoring order costs nothing. Refinement cost depends
more on disconnected mesh components than triangle count, remaining nearly
constant below approximately 100 components and rising close to linearly
thereafter, reaching 23-fold at 1,000 components. Repeating a run on the same
GPU and configuration produces byte-identical output. Component- and
integration-level correctness is established across the pipeline. Real-time
delivery, efficient large-scale mesh processing, and quantitative
reconstruction accuracy against ground truth remain future work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The recovery of three-dimensional structure from two-dimensional imagery is a
well-known problem in computer vision and computer graphics. This topic has
progressed from recent approaches to image-based 3D reconstruction \cite{b2}
and Structure-from-Motion methods \cite{b3}, through deep learning-based 3D
reconstruction from multiple images \cite{b4} and learning-based multi-view
stereo \cite{b5}, to more recent approaches exploring pose-free dense
regression with DUSt3R \cite{b6} and MASt3R \cite{b7}, as well as learned
approaches such as MVSNeRF \cite{b13}, pixelNeRF \cite{b14}, Neural Radiance
Fields \cite{b16}, Instant-NGP \cite{b20}, 3D Gaussian Splatting \cite{b21},
and Mip-NeRF 360 \cite{b22}. While each of these techniques improves upon the
ability to perform three-dimensional reconstruction, an operational system
also requires input normalization, frame selection, synchronization, geometry
refinement, and output delivery tailored to the application.

z-shift is presented as a four-stage process where individually replaceable
stages exchange data in the form of serializable artifacts. The ingestion stage
unites images, videos, synchronized captures, and live streams under a uniform
spatial representation. The reconstruction stage uses MASt3R optionally with
TSDF fusion and a fallback point-map representation, while the refinement and
delivery stages respectively handle geometry post-processing and routing of
outputs according to media type and application requirements. Thus, the focus
is on how heterogeneous media can move through the reconstruction and
refinement stages without tying the stages to any specific internal
representation.

The focus of our evaluation is on the performance of the stage interfaces. We
found that motion-ranked frame retention degrades sliding-window pairing by a
factor of 6.6 when frames are left unsorted, and that restoring capture order
recovers this at no additional cost. In contrast, refinement runtime is more
sensitive to mesh fragmentation than to the number of triangles, with the
transition occurring around 100 connected components and a 23x increase at
1,000 components in the studied configurations. Repeated runs on the same GPU
and configuration yielded byte-wise identical results.

\section{Related Work}

Several established research directions in the field of 3D reconstruction
provide well-developed techniques for geometric reconstruction from multiple
images. Recent surveys of dense 3D reconstruction describe the geometric
foundations and datasets used for image-based reconstruction \cite{b2}, while
Herrera-Granda et al.\ \cite{b3} review Structure-from-Motion, visual odometry,
and SLAM methods for 3D reconstruction. Wang et al.\ \cite{b4} survey deep
learning-based approaches for 3D reconstruction from multiple images, while
Wang et al.\ \cite{b5} consider learning-based multi-view stereo for dense
reconstruction. Machine learning has enabled many contributions in the field of
3D reconstruction with neural representations like NeRF \cite{b16},
Instant-NGP \cite{b20}, 3D Gaussian Splatting \cite{b21}, and others
\cite{b13}, \cite{b14}, \cite{b22}. DUSt3R \cite{b6} and MASt3R \cite{b7}
enable dense geometry estimation with minimal reliance on conventional pose
estimation techniques. The above-mentioned contributions mostly deal with
reconstruction problems themselves, while the organization of other stages
depends on the application at hand.

In general, reconstructed geometry needs some processing to become useful as a
resource. Yang et al.\ \cite{b1} propose a feature-driven mesh denoising
technique aimed at reducing noise while preserving important geometric
features, while Maruani et al.\ \cite{b8} present a neural mesh representation
based on Quadric Error Metrics. Surface fusion of reconstructed geometry in
volumetric representations is shown by Hu et al.\ \cite{b9} through color
truncated signed distance field fusion, while Johari et al.\ \cite{b23}
reconstruct dense surface geometry using a hybrid representation of signed
distance fields. Kazhdan and Hoppe \cite{b24} present a distributed approach to
Poisson surface reconstruction for producing surface geometry from oriented
points. Even though the above-mentioned works address geometry processing
tasks, they use different representations of input geometry, assumptions, and
parameters.

\section{Research Gap and Problem Statement}

A stage may comply with its own specification yet violate an assumption made
by the following stage. It may also introduce a cost profile that does not
appear when stages are measured separately. This paper therefore focuses on
the correctness and cost of the interfaces between stages, rather than the
validity or cost of the model used in the reconstruction stage.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

Four modular stages make up the architecture: media ingestion, multi-view
reconstruction, mesh refinement, and outcome routing. They can communicate
only through serializable artifacts, namely a unified schema, a reconstruction
job, a mesh file, and a set of manifests. These four artifacts are the
system's interfaces. They define what one stage can assume about the previous
one, and they are where the behavior documented in
Section~\ref{sec:bench-validation} originates. Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} shows the
services and artifact stores that implement these boundaries.

\subsection{Media Ingestion}

An HTTP/WebSocket gateway with pluggable authentication and rate limiting
ingests single images, image folders, videos, video folders, and live
WebSocket frames. MIME type and file extension decide which processing track a
payload enters. Mixed or unrecognized payloads are rejected outright, never
partially processed. Normalization then follows on the batch track: images are
resized while aspect ratio and EXIF metadata are preserved, and videos are
probed using FFmpeg and sampled by a motion-adaptive sampler whose interval
shortens during motion and lengthens across static spans. When the input is a
video folder, a multi-source synchronizer estimates a per-source clock offset
by matching motion signatures against the source with the most frames, then
aligns frames to that anchor by nearest timestamp. On the live track, frames
are buffered instead in a bounded ring buffer with explicit backpressure.
Oversized payloads are denied, and a full buffer evicts its oldest frame.
Compute priority goes first to live work.

\subsection{Unified Spatial Representation}

Both tracks converge into a single serializable schema, which holds source
metadata, frame references, temporal information, per-frame intrinsics, and
synchronization state. Two benefits result at this boundary between ingestion
and reconstruction. Ingestion is decoupled from reconstruction, so that each
stage encodes as little as possible about the other's internal workings. A
normalized input also becomes replayable, which lets reconstruction
experiments run against a fixed frame set instead of the original capture.

\subsection{Multi-View Reconstruction}
\label{sec:reconstruction}

An explicit reconstruction job is created during the reconstruction stage. The
mode of operation depends on the type of input used: an image folder is
processed as multi-view, a single video as a video-sequence, and a folder of
videos as synchronized-views. Since alignment requires at least two views of
one subject, single images and live streams are rejected. For video jobs, the
system treats adjacent frames as potentially redundant and applies a
configurable frame budget to each job. Above that budget the highest-motion
frames are retained in preference to a uniform subsample. When processing
synchronized captures, the budget applies to entire groups rather than to
individual frames, so cross-camera groupings stay intact. Pairing then occurs
either through a complete scene graph or by sliding window, the latter
preferred for videos and larger image sets. On synchronized-views jobs,
cross-camera pairings form within each group, so that simultaneous
observations are matched directly. Frames then proceed to pairing in
motion-rank order, not capture order. Section~\ref{sec:bench-validation}
evaluates this cross-stage interaction.

Before alignment, the pipeline selects an execution device among CUDA, CPU,
and Metal backends, seeds the Python, NumPy, and PyTorch generators if a seed
has been supplied, and derives an approximate pinhole-intrinsic initialization
from the EXIF 35~mm-equivalent focal length \cite{b10}, assuming square
pixels, a centered principal point, and no lens distortion. The MASt3R backend
then performs sparse global alignment using its official implementation
\cite{b11}, with intermediate results cached to disk so that repeated runs
over the same frames reuse prior work. TSDF fusion may optionally precede
geometry export. If fusion is disabled by a user option, or fails under memory
or runtime pressure, the pipeline falls back to a dense point-map export and
records the event. GLB is the default output format for the colored mesh, with
OBJ and PLY also supported, and a confidence threshold discards low-confidence
points. A confidence-masked dense point cloud is written alongside it.
Recorded in the run manifest are the model, device, input resolution, pairing
strategy, thresholds, seed, and reproducibility metadata.

\subsection{Mesh Refinement}

To avoid operator and threshold selection on a per-model basis, a fixed
refinement procedure is applied to every mesh. The validation phase starts by
unwrapping container formats such as GLB, and rejects empty meshes and meshes
whose vertices have non-finite coordinates. The mode decides how components
are processed further. In object mode, only the largest connected component
remains. In room mode, the mesh is split into bodies, and components that meet
a minimum cell-count threshold are selected and merged.

Boundary holes are closed up to a size derived from the bounding-box diagonal,
but for slender geometry that is open along only one axis, hole closure is
skipped. Taubin's alternating low-pass filter is used to reduce surface noise
while avoiding the mesh shrinkage characteristic of Laplacian smoothing. In
room mode, feature edges above a configurable angle are preserved. The
finalization step merges vertices, triangulates, decimates towards the target
reduction, and recomputes consistent normals.

Smoothing and decimation change the vertex set, so vertex colors are mapped to
the new mesh by nearest-point transfer. Open boundary edges are counted to
check the watertightness of the mesh. A non-watertight result is reported to
the user as a warning rather than stopping pipeline execution.

\subsection{Application-Specific Deliverables}

Compatibility between source type and use case is treated as a contract at
the outcomes stage, rather than as a default behavior. Each use case declares
the source types it can meaningfully consume. Every other combination is
rejected with a routing error before anything is exported. All 24 combinations
route or reject correctly in our benchmark, as shown in Table~\ref{tab3}.

A Blender-ready GLB leaves through the editing pathway. The viewing pathway
packages the confidence-masked point cloud into a colored PLY, rather than
producing a full Gaussian Splatting representation \cite{b21}. Defined at the
routing level, the live pathway explicitly rejects requests.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Computational Performance}
\label{sec:comp-performance}

Per-component processing overhead dominates refinement cost in the current
implementation, while triangle count has a smaller effect. When considered
alone on a single connected mesh, triangle count shows mild super-linear
growth. A log-log fit over 50K to 2.5M triangles gives an exponent of 1.16 to
1.31 across the eight configuration and geometry combinations, with
$R^2 > 0.98$ throughout.

The default configuration adds no more than 23\% over the smoothing-off
baseline. The stronger driver is component count. At a fixed $\sim$96,000
triangles, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:refine-scaling}(a), cost grows 23x between 1
and 1,000 components on pooled means, and 17--33x across the four
configurations.

Nor is this growth a clean power law. The endpoint ratio alone would overstate
how evenly the cost accumulates. Fitted over the full component ladder, a
single log-log exponent of 0.39--0.48 (0.43 pooled) fits poorly, with
$R^2 = 0.85$, because the curve has a knee. Its local exponent is 0.22 from 1
to 100 components and 0.94 from 100 to 1,000.

Below roughly 100 components, fragmentation is nearly free, rising from
0.45~s to 1.23~s, under 3x over two decades. Above this point, cost rises
close to linearly with component count, supplying the remaining $\sim$8.5x.
Fragmentation therefore behaves as a threshold rather than a gradient.

Table~\ref{tab-stage-profile} attributes this cost to different substeps in
each regime. Finalization dominates on a large, connected mesh, at 53.7\%,
mostly point deduplication and normal recomputation. On a many-component mesh,
component filtering dominates instead, at 95.9\%, driven by repeated per-piece
processing during body splitting. A mechanical explanation for the knee
follows. Body splitting runs each connected component through a separate
processing pass, so once component count grows large enough for that loop to
dominate the fixed per-mesh work, cost tracks component count directly. The
obvious optimization is a batched connected-component labeling pass, which we
did not implement for this study.

This cost belongs to an interface, not to a stage. What sets refinement's
runtime is a structural property of the mesh that the reconstruction stage
happens to hand it, and no benchmark of refinement on a single connected mesh
would reveal it. The TSDF stage similarly has an explicit fallback to dense
point-map export when fusion exceeds available resources, at the price of an
output representation that depends on those resources.

\subsection{Determinism and Repeat-Run Behavior}
\label{sec:repeat-behavior}

Run manifests record the reconstruction model, device, pairing strategy, seed,
and configuration, which supports repeatable re-execution on the same
hardware. On three DTU scenes \cite{b19} (\texttt{scan24}, \texttt{scan37},
\texttt{scan40}), a small GPU benchmark evaluated this, running each twice on
one Tesla~T4 under one seed with the cache cleared between runs. Hausdorff-95
and Chamfer distance between repeats were exactly 0.0, point counts matched,
and exported GLB files were byte-identical by SHA-256; this held regardless of
PyTorch's deterministic-algorithms mode, which added $\sim$21\% wall time
without measurably improving repeatability here. PyTorch does not guarantee
identical results across releases, platforms, or CPU and GPU executions
\cite{b29}, so reproducibility in the general sense is not established by six
runs on one device. What they show is narrower: the recorded manifest was
sufficient for bit-identical reruns in this tested environment, and enabling
deterministic algorithms did not improve repeatability in these trials.

\subsection{Component and Integration Correctness}
\label{sec:bench-validation}

Controlled validation of the individual components and their interfaces is
summarized in Table~\ref{tab3}, where every number traces to a committed CSV.
These are contract checks on a synthetic, single-body protocol. Component
filtering removes every injected fragment, and object and room mode converge
to the same output, as they should on a single body and would not on real
multi-body scenes. As injected corruption is removed, distance to the clean
ground truth improves monotonically (Fig.~\ref{fig:refine-quality}(a)--(b)),
which the injection protocol guarantees by construction. The informative
comparison there is between smoothing operators: Taubin holds mesh volume
within 0.15\% of the unsmoothed baseline, against 0.52\% drift for Laplacian
(Fig.~\ref{fig:refine-quality}(c)). Synchronization, motion-sampling, routing,
and color-transfer correctness are established by the remaining rows.

\subsubsection{Frame Ordering and Pairing Locality}
Motion-ranked retention above the frame budget
(Section~\ref{sec:reconstruction}) changes frame ordering before
sliding-window pairing, which assumes capture order. Re-sorting the retained
frames into capture order reduces the median pair-index gap from 184.5 to 27.8
across the three over-budget schedules. That is 6.6x pooled and 4.4x to 10x
per schedule, at no additional cost, and complete pairing is unaffected
because it does not depend on order. On its own, neither stage is incorrect:
selection returns the frames it was asked for, and pairing windows the
sequence it is given. Only when the two are put together does the defect
appear, and no test that exercises either stage alone would find it. Its
effect on reconstruction quality we do not measure.

\subsection{Illustrative End-to-End Trace}

This subsection adds a single traced run ($n=1$) of the offline workflow in
Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} on a real eight-view CO3D \emph{teddybear} capture
\cite{b15}. We include it to show the assembled pipeline running end to end at
full scale and to check whether the model in
Section~\ref{sec:comp-performance} predicts where the time goes. One run
supports neither a timing claim nor a quality claim. Table~\ref{tab2} reports
the per-stage timings for this purpose only.

Alignment peaked at 5259.6 MB resident and retained 64.3\% of points after
confidence masking. Total runtime was $\approx$78 min. Component filtering
alone accounted for 99.9\% of refinement wall time, while every other substep
took under one second individually and about 2~s combined (Table~\ref{tab2}).
This matches the fragmentation regime described in
Section~\ref{sec:comp-performance} at full scale. The mesh has 1.90M triangles
and is far past the component-count knee, where the benchmark predicts
component filtering will dominate the remaining stages. It does. The output was
correctly flagged as non-watertight, and the warning did not halt the
workflow. The pipeline therefore reports geometry defects instead of
terminating on them.

\section{Conclusions}

This paper presents a system architecture of independently replaceable stages
communicating through explicit artifacts (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}), and not a
new reconstruction network. Its findings concern how those stages fit
together. Correct at the selection stage, frame ordering nonetheless breaks
pairing locality at the next one, and restoring capture order recovers a 6.6x
pair-index gap for free. Mesh fragmentation, a structural property of what
reconstruction hands refinement, governs refinement cost in this
implementation far more than triangle count does, and it does so as a
threshold rather than a gradient: cost is nearly flat below roughly 100
components, then close to linear in component count, reaching 23x at 1,000.
That shape, more than the endpoint ratio, is what makes cost predictable in
advance, since a single structural property of the incoming mesh determines
which regime a run lands in. Repeat runs of a fixed configuration on one GPU
were deterministic down to the byte, with identical SHA-256 mesh hashes.

None of this would surface from a benchmark of a single stage on its own.
Component correctness does not imply system correctness or predictable system
cost. Outside this evaluation, there remains end-to-end reconstruction
accuracy against captured ground truth (Section~\ref{sec:future-work}).

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

Three principal limitations attach to the prototype. Real-time delivery is
incomplete: WebSocket ingestion works, but RTSP/WebRTC and the live output
route are unimplemented, and viewing exports a colored point cloud instead of
Gaussian Splatting. Refinement is CPU-bound and expensive on fragmented
meshes. There is no end-to-end ground-truth reconstruction benchmark.
Reconstruction requires at least two views, and authentication, rate limiting,
and streaming state remain in-memory and single-process, reflecting
research-prototype status.

Component-, integration-, and systems-level behavior is therefore what our
evaluation establishes, not reconstruction accuracy. Quantitative comparison
against classical MVS, DUSt3R, and MASt3R baselines, using Chamfer distance,
point-to-surface error, and F-score, is the priority for future work.
