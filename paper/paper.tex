\begin{abstract}
DUSt3R and MASt3R recover dense scene geometry from 2D images without camera
poses or calibration, but a deployable 2D-to-3D system also needs ingestion,
frame selection, synchronization, geometry refinement, and
application-specific delivery to work together. We present z-shift, a
four-stage pipeline of replaceable stages exchanging only serializable
artifacts. Ingestion unifies image folders, videos, and synchronized
multi-camera captures under a common spatial schema, and buffers live WebSocket
frames on a separate track. Reconstruction uses MASt3R with optional Truncated
Signed Distance Function (TSDF) fusion and a point-map fallback, refinement
applies a fixed sequence of operations, and delivery routes GLB or point-cloud
output under a compatibility contract. We evaluate with a constrained benchmark
suite, one real capture traced through every stage, and five DTU scans scored
against ground truth. Motion-ranked frame retention degrades sliding-window
pairing locality 6.7-fold unless capture order is restored, which costs nothing.
Refinement cost depends more on disconnected mesh components than on triangle
count, staying nearly flat below about 100 components and reaching 20-fold at
1,000; batched labeling removes that dependence. Repeat runs were
byte-identical on each of two GPUs. Against DTU ground truth, ten views score
3.3--8.6~mm overall, and neither 49 views, restored capture order, nor
refinement improved scores consistently. Object isolation preserves F-score on
four of five scans while removing most of the mesh, and DUSt3R runs as a second
backend with every other stage unchanged. Reconstruction from a single image or
a live stream, which the pipeline rejects, real-time delivery, and efficient
large-scale mesh processing remain future work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recovering 3D structure from 2D imagery has progressed from image-based
reconstruction \cite{b2} and Structure-from-Motion \cite{b3}, through
learning-based multi-view methods \cite{b4}, \cite{b5}, to pose-free dense
regression with DUSt3R \cite{b6} and MASt3R \cite{b7}. An operational system
also requires input normalization, frame selection, synchronization, geometry
refinement, and delivery tailored to the application.

z-shift is a four-stage process whose individually replaceable stages exchange
serializable artifacts. Ingestion unites image folders, videos, and synchronized
captures, and buffers live streams, under a uniform spatial representation;
reconstruction accepts the multi-view inputs and rejects single images and live
streams.

Our evaluation targets the stage interfaces: how frame ordering affects pairing,
how fragmentation drives refinement cost, whether repeat runs are reproducible,
and how the assembled pipeline scores against DTU ground truth \cite{b19}.

\section{Related Work}

Several established research directions in the field of 3D reconstruction
provide well-developed techniques for geometric reconstruction from multiple
images. Recent surveys of dense 3D reconstruction describe the geometric
foundations and datasets used for image-based reconstruction \cite{b2}, while
Herrera-Granda et al.\ \cite{b3} review Structure-from-Motion, visual odometry,
and SLAM methods for 3D reconstruction. Wang et al.\ \cite{b4} survey deep
learning-based approaches for 3D reconstruction from multiple images, while
Wang et al.\ \cite{b5} consider learning-based multi-view stereo for dense
reconstruction. Machine learning has enabled many contributions in the field of
3D reconstruction with neural representations like NeRF \cite{b16},
Instant-NGP \cite{b20}, 3D Gaussian Splatting \cite{b21}, and others
\cite{b13}, \cite{b14}, \cite{b22}. DUSt3R \cite{b6} and MASt3R \cite{b7}
enable dense geometry estimation with minimal reliance on conventional pose
estimation techniques. The above-mentioned contributions mostly deal with
reconstruction problems themselves, while the organization of other stages
depends on the application at hand.

In general, reconstructed geometry needs some processing to become useful as a
resource. Yang et al.\ \cite{b1} propose a feature-driven mesh denoising
technique aimed at reducing noise while preserving important geometric
features, while Maruani et al.\ \cite{b8} present a neural mesh representation
based on Quadric Error Metrics. Surface fusion of reconstructed geometry in
volumetric representations is shown by Hu et al.\ \cite{b9} through color
truncated signed distance field fusion, while Johari et al.\ \cite{b23}
reconstruct dense surface geometry using a hybrid representation of signed
distance fields. Kazhdan and Hoppe \cite{b24} present a distributed approach to
Poisson surface reconstruction for producing surface geometry from oriented
points. Even though the above-mentioned works address geometry processing
tasks, they use different representations of input geometry, assumptions, and
parameters.

\section{Research Gap and Problem Statement}

A stage may comply with its own specification yet violate an assumption made
by the following stage. It may also introduce a cost profile that does not
appear when stages are measured separately. This paper therefore focuses on
the correctness and cost of the interfaces between stages, rather than the
validity or cost of the model used in the reconstruction stage.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

Four stages (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}) communicate only through serializable
artifacts: a unified schema, a reconstruction job, a mesh file, and a set of
manifests.

\subsection{Media Ingestion}

An HTTP/WebSocket gateway ingests single images, image folders, videos, video
folders, and live WebSocket frames. MIME type and extension select the processing
track, and mixed or unrecognized payloads are rejected outright. On the batch track,
images are resized and videos are sampled by a motion-adaptive sampler. For a video
folder, a multi-source synchronizer estimates a per-source clock offset by matching
motion signatures, then aligns frames by nearest timestamp. On the live track,
frames enter a bounded ring buffer. Both tracks converge into one serializable
schema, which decouples ingestion from reconstruction and makes an input replayable.

\subsection{Multi-View Reconstruction}
\label{sec:reconstruction}

An image folder runs as multi-view, a single video as a video-sequence, and a folder
of videos as synchronized-views. Alignment needs at least two views of one subject,
so single images and live streams are rejected. Above a configurable frame budget,
video jobs retain the highest-motion frames, and synchronized captures are budgeted
by group. Pairing uses a complete scene graph or a sliding window. Frames proceed to
pairing in motion-rank order, not capture order, an interaction evaluated in
Section~\ref{sec:bench-validation}.

Before alignment, the pipeline selects a device, seeds its generators if a seed is
supplied, and derives approximate intrinsics from the EXIF focal length \cite{b10}.
MASt3R then performs sparse global alignment using its official implementation
\cite{b11}, with results cached to disk. TSDF fusion
may precede export; if it is disabled or fails, the pipeline falls back to a dense
point map and records the event. The output is a colored GLB mesh and a
confidence-masked point cloud, and the run manifest records the model, device,
resolution, pairing strategy, thresholds, and seed. The stage sits behind a backend
interface selected per job, so another model can replace MASt3R
(Section~\ref{sec:further}).

\subsection{Mesh Refinement}

A fixed procedure is applied to every mesh. Object mode then keeps the largest connected component, while
room mode merges components above a minimum cell count. Boundary holes are closed,
Taubin smoothing reduces noise without Laplacian shrinkage, finalization merges,
decimates, and recomputes normals, and vertex colors are transferred by nearest
point. A non-watertight result is reported as a warning, not an error.

\subsection{Application-Specific Deliverables}

Each use case declares the source types it can consume, and every other combination
is rejected before export; all 24 combinations route or reject correctly
(Table~\ref{tab3}). Editing emits a GLB and viewing a colored PLY point cloud rather
than Gaussian Splatting \cite{b21}. The live route rejects requests.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Computational Performance}
\label{sec:comp-performance}

The as-submitted refinement splits the mesh into bodies on the CPU. Timings here
are means over three repeats (five for Table~\ref{tab-stage-profile}) on an Intel
Core i7-14650HX laptop (24 logical cores) under WSL2. On a single connected mesh,
a log-log fit over 50K to 2.5M triangles gives an exponent of 1.10--1.21 across
eight configuration and geometry combinations ($R^2 \geq 0.98$).

Component count is the stronger driver. At a fixed $\sim$96,000 triangles
(Fig.~\ref{fig:refine-scaling}(a)), cost grows 20x between 1 and 1,000 components. The curve has a knee: the local exponent is 0.15 from 1 to 100 components
(0.15~s to 0.31~s) and 0.98 from 100 to 1,000. Fragmentation therefore behaves as
a threshold rather than a gradient. Table~\ref{tab-stage-profile} shows why:
finalization dominates on a large connected mesh, while on a fragmented one
component filtering takes 96.5\%, because body splitting runs each component
through a separate pass.

This predicts its own fix, which we implemented: one batched connected-component
labeling pass on the GPU replaces the per-body loop with identical cell and point
counts on every scaling configuration. Cost no longer depends on component count
(0.13~s at one component, 0.09~s at 1,000), and the slowest combined-load case
falls from 34~s to 4.2~s. The cost belongs to an interface, not a stage, and no
benchmark of refinement on a single connected mesh would reveal it.

\subsection{Determinism and Repeat-Run Behavior}
\label{sec:repeat-behavior}

Three DTU scenes \cite{b19} at
8 views each ran twice under one seed with the cache cleared, with PyTorch's
deterministic mode on and off, on a Tesla~T4 and on an RTX~5060 laptop GPU (8~GB;
WSL2, CUDA~13, PyTorch~2.13). On both, repeats were identical regardless of the mode
(Table~\ref{tab:quality-repro}). PyTorch does not guarantee identical results across
releases or platforms \cite{b29}, and output was not compared across the two
machines; the recorded manifest sufficed for bit-identical reruns on each tested
environment.

\subsection{Illustrative End-to-End Trace}
\label{sec:trace}

We traced the workflow on a real CO3D \emph{teddybear} capture \cite{b15} at eight
evenly spaced frames, as submitted, and at 24 (Table~\ref{tab2}). Each is a single
run, which supports neither a timing nor a quality claim.

At eight views, per-body filtering took 99.97\% of refinement, as the benchmark
predicts far past the knee. Batched labeling returns identical counts on the same
mesh in 0.31~s.

Tripling the views exposed a defect the benchmarks could not. Object mode assumes
the subject is the largest thing reconstructed, but a real capture also reconstructs
the support surface. At 24 views the floor was a separate, larger piece, and the
refined mesh was floor alone, while every stage met its own contract. We therefore
added an optional object-isolation step, off by default: it removes the dominant
RANSAC plane when that plane holds at least 30\% of the faces (69\% here), then keeps
the largest remaining piece and the fragments inside its bounding box
(Fig.~\ref{fig:validation}).
Holes and
silhouette streaks remain; Section~\ref{sec:dtu} measures accuracy.

\subsection{Reconstruction Accuracy on DTU}
\label{sec:dtu}

We score five scans against DTU's structured-light ground truth \cite{b19} using
DTU's evaluation, with overall error the mean of accuracy and completeness, distances
over 20~mm ignored, and F-score at $\tau = 2$~mm. Runs use 512-pixel input on the
RTX~5060.

MVS methods are normally given DTU's cameras; MASt3R estimates its own. Each
reconstruction is therefore placed in DTU's frame by a similarity fit from its
estimated camera centers to the calibrated ones. The fit is trimmed: a camera
whose residual exceeds both 10~mm and three times the median is left out. A run
is flagged as a pose failure if the remaining RMSE exceeds 10~mm or more than 20\%
of its cameras are left out.

Table~\ref{tab:dtu} gives the results; means include flagged runs. Ten spread
views with complete pairing score 3.30--8.59~mm overall in 157~s on average.
Forty-nine views, on a sliding window of 9, take 7.4x as long and do not reliably improve on this: overall
error is lower on two of five scans and F-score higher only on S1. Much of the
error is placement: at ten views, rigid ICP against the ground truth, which a
deployment could not run, lowers mean overall error to 3.64~mm and raises mean
F-score to 0.422. Overall error can also flatter a failed run, since it ignores
distant points: S2 at 49 views drops 37\% of its reconstructed points and 53\%
of the ground truth. Pose failures affect 3 of these 10 runs, including both on
S2.

The three seeds reach only TSDF sampling, so the sample is five scans rather than
15 runs.
Restoring capture order showed no benefit, as V2 beats V1 on one scan of five. Nor
does this show that motion-rank order is better: depending on whether the fit is
trimmed and ICP applied, V1 wins between two and five scans on overall error, even
five of five would be only $p = 0.06$ on a two-sided sign test, the trim was chosen
post hoc, and 10 of the 15 cells are pose failures.

Sampling mesh surfaces at ten views with isolation off, refinement lowers scores
on these real scans (Table~\ref{tab:quality-repro}), and completeness worsens on
every scan in object mode. Refinement removes the damage we inject in the synthetic
benchmark, but that does not make a real reconstruction more accurate.

\subsection{Further Evaluation}
\label{sec:further}

\emph{Refinement on real meshes.} On the raw meshes (1.3--2.6M cells), batched
labeling takes 0.30--0.84~s (SD at most 0.09~s over five repeats). The per-body
filter finished within a 30-min budget only on S2, in 1,259~s with the same
output, about 4,000x slower. Table~\ref{tab:isolation} shows what each variant keeps, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:validation} shows two of the five scans. Object isolation
cuts the support plane (32--60\% of faces), keeps 15--43\% of faces on four scans,
and stays within 0.001 of or above the raw mesh's F-score on them (mean 0.239
against 0.229 raw), while overall error is level on two and up to 0.89~mm higher
on two. On S2, a pose failure throughout Table~\ref{tab:dtu}, its score
collapses. The plain largest-component rule lowers F-score on three of five scans.

\emph{CO3D against its reference cloud.} CO3D's cloud is a COLMAP reconstruction
rather than independent ground truth, and the single-sequence release has no
cameras, so we register each result to it by a fixed scale and rigid ICP (residual
1.4--1.5\% of the bounding-box diagonal) and treat it as a pseudo-reference. At
F@2\% of the diagonal, the refined, isolated mesh reaches 0.745 at eight views and
0.769 at 24, against 0.586 and 0.502 for the raw point cloud. Registration favors
any well-shaped surface, so these figures compare variants, not methods.

\emph{Synchronization on real images.} The production motion score is computed on real DTU
frames and a second camera's on its own perturbed pixels, with clocks shifted by
0--640~ms over 1,100 trials. DTU's cameras carry no timestamps, so clocks are
injected, and this is not a recording with true clocks. Median absolute error is 12--30~ms per scan
against a 40~ms frame interval, the sign is correct in 84--97\% of nonzero-offset trials, and
grouping reaches 83--90\%. Mean error (31--145~ms) is larger because a minority
of trials fail by much more than the median.

\emph{A second backend.} DUSt3R \cite{b6} ran through the backend interface on the ten views of
Table~\ref{tab:dtu}. On all five scans the stage wrote the same artifacts and
refinement consumed the mesh unchanged. The swap also moves the measurement: DUSt3R
reaches mean F-score 0.069 and 8.3~mm overall against 0.210 and 5.85~mm for MASt3R,
with all five scans flagged as pose failures, in 64~s per scan against 157~s. The
reconstruction stage can thus be replaced and compared under one protocol; we make
no such claim for other stages.

\section{Conclusions}

z-shift is a system architecture of replaceable stages exchanging explicit
artifacts, not a new reconstruction network. Frame ordering that is correct at
selection breaks pairing locality at the next stage, and mesh fragmentation governs
refinement cost as a threshold, which batched labeling removes. None of this
surfaces from a benchmark of one stage. Nor does a synthetic gain imply a real one:
against DTU ground truth, the pipeline scores 3.3--8.6~mm overall at ten views, much
of it placement error, and restoring capture order, adding views, and refinement
each failed to improve scores consistently.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

Real-time delivery is incomplete: WebSocket ingestion works, but RTSP/WebRTC and
the live output route are unimplemented, and viewing exports a colored point
cloud. Most of refinement stays on the CPU. The ground-truth benchmark covers five
scans of one dataset at 512-pixel input, camera pose fails on 3 of its 10
view-count runs and 10 of its 15 frame-ordering cells, and no video with ground
truth was tested. 

No classical baseline ran under the same protocol, and published MVS scores, which
are given DTU's cameras, are not comparable. More robust camera estimation and
comparison against classical MVS baselines under one protocol are the priorities
for future work.
