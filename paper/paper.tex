\begin{abstract}
Unposed learned methods such as DUSt3R and MASt3R now recover dense geometry
from two-dimensional observations without calibration or pose, but a deployable
2D-to-3D system needs more than a reconstruction network. It also needs
ingestion, frame selection, temporal synchronization, geometric refinement, and
application-specific delivery, and those stages have to work together. We present z-shift, a four-stage pipeline whose stages are
independently replaceable and communicate only through serializable artifacts.
Ingestion normalizes images, videos, synchronized captures, and live frames into
a unified spatial schema. Reconstruction wraps MASt3R with optional TSDF fusion
and a point-map fallback. Refinement applies a fixed operator sequence.
Delivery routes GLB or point-cloud outputs under a compatibility contract. We
evaluate the pipeline on a controlled benchmark suite and trace one eight-view
capture end to end. Motion-ranked frame retention degrades sliding-window
pairing locality by 6.6x unless capture order is restored, which costs nothing
to fix. Refinement cost is governed by mesh fragmentation more than by triangle
count, and behaves as a threshold: nearly flat below roughly 100 components,
then close to linear, reaching 23x at 1,000. Repeat runs of one configuration
on one GPU were byte-identical. We establish component- and integration-level
correctness across the pipeline. Real-time delivery, efficient large-scale mesh
processing, and quantitative reconstruction accuracy against ground truth remain
future work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recovering three-dimensional structure from two-dimensional images is a fundamental
problem in computer vision and graphics. Classical structure-from-motion (SfM) and
multi-view stereo (MVS) methods estimate camera geometry, correspondences, depth, and
surfaces from multiple observations \cite{b1}--\cite{b4}, but are sensitive to
viewpoint coverage, image quality, and correspondence reliability. Learning-based
methods have since expanded these capabilities: Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)
introduced a continuous neural scene representation for view synthesis \cite{b6},
with follow-on work on faster rendering \cite{b7}, explicit representations
\cite{b8}, large-scale \cite{b9}, and generalized reconstruction \cite{b10},
\cite{b11}. DUSt3R introduced dense 3D reconstruction from image pairs without a
conventional calibrated pipeline \cite{b14}, and MASt3R strengthened this through
improved image matching \cite{b15}.

Once a capable reconstruction engine is available, a question remains: how should
the surrounding stages be arranged around it so that heterogeneous raw media
reach a usable deliverable at predictable cost, reproducibly? z-shift is one
answer, a fixed sequence of independently replaceable stages that communicate
only through serializable artifacts, with MASt3R occupying the reconstruction
slot. Our evaluation supports a single claim. Individually correct stages do not
compose into a correct or predictable system for free, and the interfaces
between them carry their own correctness and cost behavior.

Real-world input is heterogeneous. Photographs, image directories, videos,
synchronized multi-camera captures, and continuous frame streams all arrive at
the same entry point, and video adds redundancy and cost. z-shift normalizes
these sources into a common spatial schema and bounds the frames entering
reconstruction (Section~\ref{sec:architecture}). Raw reconstruction output is
not production-ready either. Surfaces may contain disconnected components,
holes, noise, and inconsistent normals. Established mesh-processing methods
address all four \cite{b16}--\cite{b19}, and z-shift applies them automatically
in a dedicated refinement stage that preserves vertex colors.

We contribute five systems abstractions. (1) A unified ingestion schema that
decouples heterogeneous batch and live media from everything downstream and
makes a normalized input replayable. (2) Bounded orchestration of frame
selection and pairing under an explicit frame budget. (3) A backend layer
wrapping MASt3R with optional TSDF fusion and a recorded point-map fallback.
(4) An automated geometry-processing stage that applies a fixed refinement
sequence without per-model human choices. (5) A delivery layer that enforces
source-type and use-case compatibility as a contract and emits reproducibility
manifests.

We also report three empirical findings about how the stages interact. Frame
ordering degrades pairing locality by 6.6x, and the fix is free. Mesh
fragmentation governs refinement cost more strongly than triangle count does,
and it does so as a threshold near 100 components, not a smooth gradient.
Repeat runs of a fixed configuration on one GPU are byte-identical, so a run is
reproducible from its manifest when hardware is held fixed.

\section{Related Work}

Classical SfM and MVS recover camera poses and dense geometry from
correspondences across images \cite{b1}--\cite{b4}, but degrade on textureless,
specular, or sparsely covered surfaces. Learned scene representations relax that
dependence. NeRF and its accelerated, explicit, and unbounded-scene successors
\cite{b6}--\cite{b9}, along with generalizable one- and few-view variants
\cite{b10}, \cite{b11}, optimize for view synthesis. What they do not produce is
an explicit exportable surface, which limits their direct use as an
asset-production backend.

DUSt3R \cite{b14} and MASt3R \cite{b15} are the most directly relevant prior
work. Both regress dense per-pixel point maps in a common reference frame from
unposed image pairs, which removes calibration and pose as prerequisites, and
MASt3R adds a dense local-feature head that improves matching. The output of
each is a confidence-weighted point map, not a clean surface, which is what
motivates the refinement stage described later. z-shift adopts MASt3R as its
current backend. Since stages communicate only through serializable artifacts
(Section~\ref{sec:architecture}), MASt3R could be swapped for a comparable
method without changing anything around it. The volumetric fusion
\cite{b21}--\cite{b23} and mesh-processing \cite{b16}--\cite{b19} methods we use
are likewise established. Our contribution is the assembled system and what can
be measured about it: which interface properties determine its cost, and what a
run must record to be reproducible.

z-shift belongs to a smaller literature of complete reconstruction \emph{systems}
rather than individual algorithms. COLMAP chains incremental SfM \cite{b2} with
dense multi-view stereo \cite{b25}, and AliceVision's Meshroom \cite{b26} wraps a
similar chain in a node-based GUI built for interactive, human-supervised use.
Nerfstudio \cite{b27} standardizes data loading, training, and export for
radiance-field methods, while Open3D \cite{b28} and Kaolin \cite{b29} supply
reusable primitives and leave the ingestion-to-delivery workflow to the user. z-shift
departs from the classical pipelines by adopting an unposed learned backend in
place of calibrated SfM/MVS. It departs from all four by targeting a fixed,
non-interactive path from heterogeneous raw media through automatic refinement
to use-case-routed export, instead of an interactive authoring tool or a library
the user must assemble.

Two consequences follow. Mesh quality is conventionally restored by a human
choosing operators and thresholds per model \cite{b18}, \cite{b19}, so an
automatic pipeline has to fix the sequence in advance. Its cost then becomes a
property of the input's structure and not of an operator's judgment.
Reconstruction accuracy, meanwhile, is conventionally reported against captured
ground truth on datasets such as ScanNet \cite{b34}. Those protocols evaluate a
reconstruction method on its own. They say nothing about whether the surrounding
stages preserve the properties a downstream tool requires, which is the gap our
benchmark addresses.

\section{Research Gap and Problem Statement}

The reconstruction components surveyed above are well studied, and on their own
they work. Deployment additionally requires the surrounding stages enumerated in
Section~\ref{sec:architecture}, and assembling them is a distinct problem, since
a stage that satisfies its own specification can still violate an assumption the
next stage makes, or show a cost profile that no stage exhibits alone. We
address the correctness and cost of the interfaces between those stages. That is
a separate question from the accuracy of whichever model occupies the
reconstruction slot.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

The architecture has four modular stages: media ingestion, multi-view
reconstruction, mesh refinement, and outcome routing. They communicate only
through serializable artifacts, namely a unified schema, a reconstruction job, a
mesh file, and a set of manifests. Those four artifacts are the system's
interfaces. Each defines what one stage may assume about the previous one, and
each is where the behavior reported in Section~\ref{sec:bench-validation}
originates. Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} summarizes the flow.

\subsection{Media Ingestion}

Ingestion accepts single images, image folders, videos, video folders, and live
WebSocket frames through an HTTP/WebSocket gateway with pluggable authentication
and rate limiting. A classifier selects a processing track from MIME type and file
extension. Mixed or unrecognized payloads are rejected outright, never partially
processed. The batch track then normalizes uploads: images are resized with
aspect ratio and EXIF metadata preserved, while videos are probed with FFmpeg and
sampled by a motion-adaptive sampler that shortens its interval during motion and
lengthens it across static spans. For video folders, a multi-source
synchronizer estimates a per-source clock offset by matching motion signatures
against the source with the most frames, then aligns frames to that anchor by
nearest timestamp. The live track instead buffers frames in a bounded ring buffer
with explicit backpressure. Oversized payloads are refused and a full buffer
evicts its oldest frame. Live work receives top compute priority.

\subsection{Unified Spatial Representation}

Both tracks converge into a single serializable schema holding source metadata,
frame references, temporal information, per-frame intrinsics, and synchronization
state. This is the pipeline's first artifact boundary, and it does two things.
It decouples ingestion from reconstruction, so neither stage encodes assumptions
about the other's internals. It also makes a normalized input replayable, so
reconstruction experiments run against a fixed frame set instead of the original
capture.

\subsection{Multi-View Reconstruction}
\label{sec:reconstruction}

The reconstruction stage converts the schema into an explicit reconstruction job.
Each source type maps to a mode: image folder to multi-view, single video to
video-sequence, video folder to synchronized-views. Single images and live
streams are rejected, since alignment requires at least two views of one subject.
Video is redundant, so the job is capped at a configurable frame budget. Above
that budget the highest-motion frames are retained in preference to a uniform
subsample, and for synchronized captures the budget applies to whole groups, so
cross-camera groups are never split. Pairing then uses either a complete scene
graph or a sliding window, the latter preferred for videos and larger image sets,
and for synchronized-views jobs cross-camera pairs are formed within each group
so simultaneous observations are matched directly. Retained frames
reach pairing in motion-rank order, not capture order. This is a cross-stage
interaction, and Section~\ref{sec:bench-validation} measures it.

The pipeline resolves an execution device
across CUDA, CPU, and Metal backends, seeds the Python, NumPy, and PyTorch generators
when a seed is supplied, and converts an EXIF 35~mm-equivalent focal length into the
per-image intrinsic matrix supplied to alignment. MASt3R then performs sparse global
alignment, caching intermediate results on disk so repeated runs over the same
frames reuse prior work. Geometry export is optionally preceded by TSDF fusion;
when fusion is disabled, or fails under memory or runtime pressure, the pipeline
falls back to a dense point-map export and records the fallback. The exporter
writes a colored mesh in GLB by default, with OBJ and PLY also supported,
applying a confidence threshold to discard low-confidence points. It also writes
a confidence-masked dense point cloud. A run manifest records the model, device,
input resolution, pairing strategy, thresholds, seed, and reproducibility
metadata.

\subsection{Mesh Refinement}

The refinement stage applies one fixed operator sequence, in place of the
per-model operator and threshold choices a human would make in an interactive
tool. It first validates its input, unwrapping container formats such as GLB
scenes and rejecting meshes that are empty or contain non-finite coordinates.
Component handling then depends on mode. Object mode retains only the largest
connected component. Room mode splits the mesh into bodies, keeps every
component above a minimum cell count, and merges the survivors, which preserves
scenes legitimately made of several pieces. Boundary holes are closed up to a
maximum size derived from the bounding-box diagonal. Closure is skipped when the
geometry is itself thin and open along one axis, so an intentionally planar
capture is not sealed.

Surfaces are smoothed with Taubin smoothing, which suppresses noise without the
shrinkage of repeated Laplacian passes; room mode additionally preserves feature
edges above a configurable angle. Finalization merges coincident points,
triangulates, optionally decimates to a target reduction, and recomputes
outward-facing normals. Smoothing and decimation rewrite the vertex set, so
vertex colors are carried onto the cleaned mesh by nearest-point transfer and
appearance survives the geometric operations. This is an invariant the interface
between these two stages must hold, and Section~\ref{sec:bench-validation}
checks it. A final pass counts open boundary edges and flags the mesh watertight
or not, treating a non-watertight result as a warning that does not terminate the
workflow. The stage emits a refinement manifest recording input and output
counts, the watertightness result, and any warnings.

\subsection{Application-Specific Deliverables}

The outcomes stage treats source-type and use-case compatibility as a contract,
not a default. Each use case declares the source types it can meaningfully
consume, and every other combination is rejected with a routing error before
anything is exported. Our benchmark (Table~\ref{tab3}) confirms all 24
combinations route or reject correctly. The editing pathway exports a
Blender-ready GLB. The viewing pathway packages the confidence-masked point
cloud into a colored PLY, short of a full Gaussian Splatting representation. The
live pathway is defined at the routing level and rejects requests explicitly.

In the current implementation, per-component processing overhead dominates
refinement cost, and triangle count matters less. On a single connected mesh,
triangle count alone is only mildly super-linear. A log-log fit over 50K--2.5M
triangles gives an exponent of 1.16--1.31 across the eight
configuration/geometry combinations, with $R^2 > 0.98$ throughout, and the
default configuration adds only 23\% over the smoothing-off baseline. Component
count is the stronger driver. Held at a fixed $\sim$96,000 triangles
(Fig.~\ref{fig:refine-scaling}(a)), cost grows 23x between 1 and 1,000
components on the pooled means, and 17--33x across the four configurations.

That growth is not a clean power law, and the endpoint ratio alone would
overstate how evenly it accumulates. A single log-log fit over the component
ladder gives an exponent of 0.39--0.48 (0.43 pooled) but fits poorly
($R^2 = 0.85$). The curve has a knee. Its local exponent is 0.22 from 1 to 100
components and 0.94 from 100 to 1,000. Below roughly 100 components,
fragmentation is nearly free: 0.45~s to 1.23~s, under 3x over two decades. Above
that point cost rises close to linearly in component count, supplying the
remaining $\sim$8.5x. Fragmentation behaves as a threshold, not a gradient.
Refinement stays cheap until component count reaches the low hundreds, past
which each additional component costs roughly a fixed amount. The two axes are
also super-additive. The combined configuration costs roughly 4x the additive
prediction and reaches the $\sim$7-minute regime marked in
Fig.~\ref{fig:refine-scaling}(b).

Table~\ref{tab-stage-profile} attributes this cost to different substeps in each
regime. On a large connected mesh, finalization dominates at 53.7\%, mostly
point deduplication and normal recomputation. On a many-component mesh,
component filtering dominates instead at 95.9\%, driven by repeated per-piece
processing during body splitting. The knee has a mechanical explanation. Body
splitting runs each connected component through a separate processing pass, so
once component count is large enough for that loop to dominate the fixed
per-mesh work, cost tracks component count directly. A batched
connected-component labeling pass is the obvious optimization. We did not
implement it for this study.

This cost belongs to an interface, not to a stage. Refinement's runtime is set
by a structural property of the mesh that the reconstruction stage happens to
hand it, and no benchmark of refinement on a single connected mesh would reveal
it. TSDF fusion can become expensive in a similar way. Its fallback to dense
point-map export degrades gracefully, at the price of an output representation
that depends on available resources.

\subsection{Determinism and Repeat-Run Behavior}

Run manifests record the reconstruction model, device, pairing strategy, seed,
and configuration, which supports repeatable re-execution on the same hardware.
A small GPU benchmark tested this on three scenes (\texttt{scan24},
\texttt{scan37}, \texttt{scan40}), each run twice on one Tesla~T4 under one seed
with the cache cleared between runs. Hausdorff-95 and Chamfer distance between
repeats were exactly 0.0, point counts matched, and exported GLB files were
byte-identical by SHA-256, and this held regardless of PyTorch's
deterministic-algorithms mode, which added $\sim$21\% wall time without
measurably improving repeatability here. Six runs on one device do not establish
reproducibility in the general sense. What they show is narrower: that the
manifest captures enough state to reproduce a run bit-for-bit when hardware is
held fixed, and that the deterministic-kernel setting is not what buys it.

\subsection{Component and Integration Correctness}
\label{sec:bench-validation}

Table~\ref{tab3} summarizes controlled validation of the individual components
and their interfaces. Every number traces to a committed CSV. These are contract
checks on a synthetic, single-body protocol. Component filtering removes every
injected fragment, and object and room mode converge to the same output, as they
should on a single body and would not on real multi-body scenes. Distance to the
clean ground truth improves monotonically as injected corruption is removed
(Fig.~\ref{fig:refine-quality}(a)--(b)), which the injection protocol largely
guarantees by construction. The informative comparison there is between
smoothing operators.
Taubin holds mesh volume within 0.15\% of the unsmoothed baseline, against
0.52\% drift for Laplacian (Fig.~\ref{fig:refine-quality}(c)). The remaining
rows establish synchronization, motion-sampling, routing, and color-transfer
correctness.

\textbf{Frame ordering and pairing locality.} Motion-ranked retention above the
frame budget (Section~\ref{sec:reconstruction}) changes frame ordering before
sliding-window pairing, which assumes capture order. Re-sorting the retained
frames into capture order reduces the median pair-index gap from 184.5 to 27.8
across the three over-budget schedules. That is 6.6x pooled and 4.4x to 10x per
schedule, at no additional cost, and complete pairing is unaffected because it
does not depend on order. Neither stage is incorrect on its own. Selection
returns the frames it was asked for, and pairing windows the sequence it is
given. The defect appears only when the two are put together, and no test that
exercises either stage alone would find it. We do not measure its effect on
reconstruction quality.

Together these establish component-level correctness against synthetic ground
truth, integration correctness across the routing, manifest, and color-transfer
invariants, and systems-level behavior under controlled conditions, covering
scaling, determinism, and robustness to injected corruption. End-to-end
reconstruction accuracy against captured ground truth is a separate question,
which the protocols in Section~\ref{sec:future-work} address.

correctly flagged as non-watertight and the warning did not halt the workflow,
so the pipeline reports geometry defects instead of terminating on them.

\section{Conclusions}

z-shift is a systems architecture of independently replaceable stages
communicating through explicit artifacts (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}), and not a new
reconstruction network. Its findings are about how those stages fit together.
Frame ordering is correct at the selection stage but breaks pairing locality at
the next one, and restoring capture order recovers a 6.6x pair-index gap for
free. Mesh fragmentation, a structural property of what reconstruction hands
refinement, governs refinement cost in this implementation far more than triangle
count does, and it does so as a threshold rather than a gradient: cost is nearly
flat below roughly 100 components, then close to linear in component count,
reaching 23x at 1,000. That shape is what makes cost predictable in advance, more than the
endpoint ratio does, because a single structural property of the incoming mesh
determines which regime a run lands in. Repeat runs of a fixed configuration on
one GPU were deterministic down to the byte, with identical SHA-256 mesh hashes.
No benchmark of a single stage on its own would surface any of this. Component
correctness does not imply system correctness or predictable system cost. What
remains outside this evaluation is end-to-end reconstruction accuracy against
captured ground truth (Section~\ref{sec:future-work}).

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

The prototype has three principal limitations. Real-time delivery is incomplete:
WebSocket ingestion works, but RTSP/WebRTC and the live outcome route are
unimplemented, and viewing exports a colored point cloud instead of Gaussian
Splatting. Refinement is CPU-bound and expensive on fragmented meshes. There is
no end-to-end ground-truth reconstruction benchmark. Reconstruction requires at
least two views, and authentication, rate limiting, and streaming state remain
in-memory and single-process, reflecting research-prototype status.

Our evaluation therefore establishes component-, integration-, and systems-level
behavior, but not reconstruction accuracy. Quantitative comparison against
classical MVS, DUSt3R, and MASt3R baselines, using Chamfer distance,
point-to-surface error, and F-score, is the priority for future work.
