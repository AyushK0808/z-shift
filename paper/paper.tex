\begin{abstract}
Recent advances in computer vision have substantially improved 3D reconstruction
from two-dimensional observations. However, practical 2D-to-3D systems must
address challenges beyond reconstruction inference, including heterogeneous input
formats, frame selection, camera metadata, temporal synchronization, computational
constraints, geometric refinement, and application-specific delivery. This paper
presents z-shift, a modular end-to-end pipeline that transforms images, image
collections, videos, synchronized video collections, and live frames into 3D
representations. The system normalizes heterogeneous inputs into a unified spatial
schema, constructs reconstruction jobs, performs multi-view reconstruction using
MASt3R, optionally applies TSDF fusion, and refines reconstructed meshes through
component filtering, hole filling, Taubin smoothing, decimation, normal correction,
and color preservation. A downstream outcomes engine generates application-specific
GLB or point-cloud deliverables. The implementation further incorporates
frame-selection strategies, synchronization, reproducibility metadata, and
computational fallbacks. The current system demonstrates functional integration from
media ingestion to refined 3D assets, while exposing limitations in real-time
delivery, large-scale mesh processing, and quantitative reconstruction evaluation.
The work provides a practical framework for integrating modern learned reconstruction
methods into a reproducible heterogeneous-media 3D generation workflow.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recovering three-dimensional structure from two-dimensional images is a fundamental
problem in computer vision and graphics. Classical structure-from-motion (SfM) and
multi-view stereo (MVS) methods estimate camera geometry, correspondences, depth, and
surfaces from multiple observations \cite{b1}--\cite{b5}. These approaches established
the geometric foundations of image-based reconstruction but can be sensitive to
viewpoint coverage, image quality, scene texture, and correspondence reliability.

Learning-based methods have considerably expanded the capabilities of 3D
reconstruction. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) introduced a continuous neural scene
representation for view synthesis \cite{b6}, while subsequent methods investigated
faster rendering \cite{b7}, explicit representations \cite{b8}, large-scale
reconstruction \cite{b9}, and generalized reconstruction \cite{b10}--\cite{b13}.
DUSt3R introduced dense 3D reconstruction from image pairs without requiring a
conventional calibrated pipeline \cite{b14}, and MASt3R strengthened this paradigm
through improved image matching and 3D correspondence \cite{b15}. These methods
provide powerful reconstruction engines, but deploying them within a practical
2D-to-3D workflow introduces additional data-management and geometric-processing
requirements.

Real-world input is heterogeneous. A system may receive photographs, image
directories, videos, synchronized multi-camera captures, or continuous frame streams.
z-shift addresses this problem through a unified ingestion architecture that
classifies input media, normalizes images and videos, extracts available camera
metadata, samples video frames, synchronizes multi-source captures, and produces a
common unified spatial ingestion schema.

Large video collections also introduce redundancy and computational cost. Rather than
processing every frame, z-shift performs motion-adaptive sampling and limits the
number of frames passed to reconstruction. It further provides complete and
sliding-window pairing strategies, favoring the latter for videos and larger image
sets.

Raw reconstruction is not necessarily a production-ready asset. Reconstructed surfaces
may contain disconnected components, holes, noise, duplicated points, and inconsistent
normals. Mesh-processing research provides established solutions for smoothing and
simplification \cite{b16}--\cite{b20}. z-shift incorporates these operations into a
dedicated refinement stage while preserving vertex colors.

The principal objective of this work is therefore to integrate established 3D vision
and geometry-processing methods into an end-to-end, reproducible pipeline rather than
propose another standalone reconstruction network. The resulting architecture connects
heterogeneous media ingestion, learned reconstruction, geometric refinement, and
application-specific delivery. In summary, this work contributes: (1) a unified
ingestion schema that normalizes heterogeneous batch and live visual media; (2) a
frame-selection and pairing strategy that bounds reconstruction cost for large image
and video collections; (3) an orchestration layer that integrates MASt3R reconstruction
with optional TSDF fusion and a robust point-map fallback; (4) a mesh-refinement stage
that produces color-preserving, application-ready geometry; and (5) an
outcomes-routing layer that generates editing- and viewing-specific deliverables,
together with reproducibility manifests.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Multi-View Reconstruction}

Classical SfM reconstructs camera poses and sparse structure from multiple images
\cite{b1}, \cite{b2}, while MVS estimates dense geometry using multiple observations
\cite{b3}--\cite{b5}. Furukawa and Hern\'andez \cite{b4} reviewed the development of
dense MVS, highlighting the importance of correspondence, visibility, and surface
consistency. Sch\"onberger and Frahm \cite{b2} demonstrated scalable SfM through robust
image matching and reconstruction. These methods remain important foundations for
modern learned systems.

Deep learning has introduced alternative representations. NeRF \cite{b6} models a
continuous radiance field, while Instant-NGP \cite{b7} substantially accelerates neural
field optimization. 3D Gaussian Splatting \cite{b8} replaces implicit fields with
explicit Gaussian primitives for efficient rendering. PixelNeRF \cite{b11}, MVSNeRF
\cite{b10}, and related generalized approaches \cite{b12}, \cite{b13} investigate
reconstruction and view synthesis across multiple scenes rather than optimizing
independently for every scene.

DUSt3R \cite{b14} and MASt3R \cite{b15} are particularly relevant to the present work
because they directly address geometric reconstruction and image matching. z-shift
employs MASt3R as its principal reconstruction engine and adds an orchestration layer
for input normalization, frame selection, pairing, synchronization, fusion, and export.

\subsection{Volumetric and Geometric Reconstruction}

Volumetric fusion is an established strategy for combining multiple depth observations.
Curless and Levoy \cite{b21} introduced signed-distance volumetric integration, while
KinectFusion \cite{b22} demonstrated real-time TSDF reconstruction. Poisson surface
reconstruction \cite{b23} provides another established approach for converting oriented
points into surfaces.

Mesh quality is equally important for downstream applications. Taubin smoothing
\cite{b16} reduces surface noise while limiting shrinkage, Garland and Heckbert
\cite{b17} introduced quadric-error simplification, and Kazhdan et al. \cite{b23}
demonstrated Poisson reconstruction. MeshLab \cite{b18} and related geometry-processing
frameworks further demonstrate the importance of post-reconstruction processing.

z-shift incorporates optional TSDF fusion followed by mesh refinement, including
component filtering, hole filling, smoothing, triangulation, optional decimation,
normal correction, and watertightness checking.

\subsection{Streaming and Representation}

Video and live reconstruction require additional mechanisms for frame sampling,
synchronization, buffering, and latency control. Streaming reconstruction systems such
as KinectFusion \cite{b22} demonstrate the feasibility of real-time processing, while
neural rendering approaches such as Gaussian Splatting \cite{b8} emphasize efficient
visualization.

z-shift currently supports WebSocket-based live frame ingestion with bounded buffering
and backpressure, but its real-time delivery pathway remains unfinished. Its
implemented viewing output is a colored PLY point cloud rather than a full Gaussian
Splatting representation.

\subsection{Automatic Rigging}

Converting reconstructed geometry into animation-ready assets requires skeleton
estimation and skinning. Baran and Popovi\'c \cite{b24} presented automatic rigging and
skinning for 3D characters, while later work investigated learning-based correspondence
and human-model reconstruction \cite{b25}--\cite{b27}. z-shift contains an auto-rigging
module supporting multiple articulation categories, and this module is wired into the
main pipeline so that a rigged asset can be produced as a deliverable.

\section{Research Gap and Problem Statement}

Existing research provides strong solutions for individual components of 3D generation:
SfM/MVS addresses geometric reconstruction \cite{b1}--\cite{b5}, neural fields and
explicit neural representations address learned scene modeling \cite{b6}--\cite{b15},
volumetric methods address depth integration \cite{b21}, \cite{b22}, and mesh-processing
techniques improve geometric quality \cite{b16}--\cite{b20}, \cite{b23}. However, these
components are commonly studied independently.

A practical 2D-to-3D system must additionally determine how heterogeneous observations
should be classified, normalized, sampled, synchronized, reconstructed, refined, and
packaged. These requirements become particularly important when inputs originate from
different sources or when computational resources are limited.

The problem addressed in this work is therefore: how can heterogeneous 2D visual media
be transformed into clean, reproducible, application-ready 3D representations through an
integrated pipeline combining learned multi-view reconstruction, data normalization,
geometric refinement, and use-case-specific delivery?

The research gap is consequently not the absence of reconstruction algorithms, but the
lack of a unified workflow connecting these algorithms to practical media ingestion and
downstream asset generation. z-shift addresses this systems-level gap through five
modular stages.

\section{System Architecture}

The z-shift architecture is organized into five modular stages: media ingestion,
multi-view reconstruction, mesh refinement, outcome routing, and automatic rigging.
The stages communicate only through explicit, serializable artifacts -- a unified
schema, a reconstruction job, a mesh file, and a set of manifests -- so that any single
stage can be replaced or evaluated in isolation. Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} summarizes the
main end-to-end flow. Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} then details ingestion and reconstruction,
and Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry} details refinement, rigging, and delivery; each stage is
described against those figures below.

\subsection{Media Ingestion}

The ingestion stage accepts single images, image folders, single videos, video folders,
and live WebSocket frames through an HTTP and WebSocket gateway with pluggable
authentication and a per-subject rate limiter. A classifier applies a decision matrix
over MIME types and file extensions to determine the source type and select a
processing track. Payloads that mix image and video content, or that contain
unrecognized files, are rejected as an unknown source type rather than being partially
processed.

The batch track normalizes uploaded files. Images are resized while preserving aspect
ratio -- frames are never padded to a square canvas or stretched -- and available EXIF
camera metadata, including make, model, and focal length, is extracted. Videos are
probed with FFmpeg and sampled by a motion-adaptive sampler that measures inter-frame
difference on downscaled grayscale frames and shortens the sampling interval during
motion while lengthening it across static spans, subject to a minimum interval between
samples.

For video folders, a multi-source synchronizer first estimates a constant clock offset
per source by matching motion signatures between each source and the source with the
most frames, then aligns frames to that anchor by nearest adjusted timestamp within a
tolerance. Groups in which every source contributes a frame become entries in a
synchronization map, so multi-camera captures remain temporally coherent downstream.

The live track buffers real-time frames in a bounded ring buffer. Every pushed frame
receives an explicit backpressure decision: oversized or undecodable payloads are
refused, and when the buffer is full the oldest frame is evicted and the drop is
reported to the producer, so a fast producer cannot exhaust memory. A latency-aware
resource tagger finally assigns each input a normalized compute-priority score, with
live streams receiving top priority and batch scores varying by source type and payload
size. The upper band of Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} illustrates the batch and live tracks
and their convergence into the unified schema.

\subsection{Unified Spatial Representation}

Both processing tracks converge into a single unified spatial ingestion schema, which
decouples ingestion from downstream reconstruction. The schema records the source type,
the track that produced it, resolution, frame count, camera intrinsics, the
compute-priority score, and a list of frame references. Each frame reference carries its
own normalized asset location, ordering index, capture timestamp, motion score,
resolution, and any per-frame intrinsics, together with the synchronization map for
multi-camera captures.

Because the schema is a self-contained, serializable representation, previously
generated ingestion runs can be persisted and replayed. Reconstruction experiments can
therefore be repeated against a fixed set of normalized frames, separating algorithmic
evaluation from the original data-capture process.

\subsection{Multi-View Reconstruction}

The reconstruction stage converts the unified schema into an explicit reconstruction
job. Each source type maps to a reconstruction mode: an image folder becomes a
multi-view job, a single video becomes a video-sequence job, and a video folder becomes
a synchronized-views job. Single images and live streams do not map to a supported mode
and are rejected, since sparse alignment requires at least two views of the same subject.

Frames are ordered deterministically by source, index, and identifier, and the job is
capped at a configurable frame budget; when the budget is exceeded, the highest-motion
frames are retained in preference to a uniform subsample. For synchronized captures the
budget is applied to whole synchronization groups, scaled by the average number of
cameras per group, so that cross-camera groups are never split.

Pairing then determines which image pairs enter alignment. A complete scene graph
compares every pair, while a sliding-window strategy bounds the pair count and is
preferred for videos and for image sets beyond a threshold size. For synchronized-views
jobs, cross-camera pairs are generated within each synchronization group so that
simultaneous observations are matched directly; if no such pairs can be resolved, the
configured strategy is used instead.

The pipeline resolves an execution device across CUDA, CPU, and Metal backends, and when
a seed is supplied it seeds the Python, NumPy, and PyTorch generators and records the
library versions. Where EXIF reports a 35~mm-equivalent focal length, it is converted
into a pixel focal length and used to initialize the per-image intrinsic matrix supplied
to alignment. MASt3R then performs sparse global alignment, caching intermediate results
on disk so repeated runs over the same frames reuse prior work.

Geometry export is optionally preceded by TSDF fusion. When fusion is disabled, or when
it fails under memory or runtime pressure, the pipeline falls back to a dense point-map
export and records the fallback in the run manifest; this is a robustness mechanism
rather than a novel fusion algorithm. The exporter writes a colored mesh -- GLB by
default, with OBJ and PLY also supported -- applying a confidence threshold to discard
low-confidence points, and additionally writes a confidence-masked dense point cloud.
A run manifest records the model, device, input resolution, pairing strategy,
thresholds, seed, and reproducibility metadata. The lower band of
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} details job construction, frame selection, pairing, alignment,
and the conditional fusion and point-map paths.

\subsection{Mesh Refinement}

The refinement stage converts raw reconstruction output into cleaner geometry. It first
validates its input, unwrapping multi-primitive container formats such as GLB scenes and
rejecting meshes that are empty or contain non-finite coordinates.

Component handling then depends on the refinement mode. Object mode retains only the
largest connected component, discarding stray fragments, while room mode splits the mesh
into bodies, keeps every component above a minimum cell count, and merges the survivors
so that scenes legitimately composed of several pieces are preserved. Boundary holes are
then closed up to a maximum size that, when not specified, is derived from the bounding
box diagonal so that the threshold scales with the model; this closure step is skipped
when the captured geometry is itself thin and open along one axis, so that an
intentionally one-sided or planar capture is not inappropriately sealed.

Surfaces are smoothed with Taubin smoothing, which suppresses noise without the
shrinkage introduced by repeated Laplacian passes; in room mode, feature edges and
boundaries above a configurable feature angle are preserved. Finalization merges
coincident points within a relative tolerance, triangulates the result, optionally
decimates it to a target reduction, and recomputes consistent outward-facing normals.

Because smoothing and decimation rewrite the vertex set, vertex color arrays are carried
onto the cleaned mesh by nearest-point transfer, so appearance survives the geometric
operations. A final check counts open boundary edges and flags the mesh as watertight or
not; a non-watertight result is reported as a warning rather than terminating the
workflow, since many downstream uses do not require a closed surface. The refined mesh
is written alongside a refinement manifest recording input and output point and cell
counts, the watertightness result, and any warnings. The upper band of
Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry} shows the complete sequence.

\subsection{Application-Specific Deliverables}

The outcomes stage routes refined geometry according to its intended use. Routing is
validated against the source type before anything is exported: the editing use case
accepts image, image-folder, video, and video-folder inputs; the viewing use case accepts
image-folder, single-video, and video-folder inputs; and the live use case accepts only
live-stream inputs. Any other combination is rejected with an explicit routing error
rather than silently falling through to a default.

The editing pathway exports a Blender-ready GLB for assets destined for a
digital-content-creation tool. The viewing pathway packages the confidence-masked point
cloud produced during reconstruction into a colored PLY. As shown in the lower band of
Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry}, this viewing deliverable is a conventional colored point cloud:
it does not encode Gaussian covariance, spherical harmonics, opacity, or a temporal
dimension, and is therefore distinct from a full Gaussian Splatting representation. A
live delivery pathway is defined at the routing level and rejects requests explicitly.

When a rigged asset is requested together with the editing use case, the skinned export
produced by the rigging stage becomes the editing deliverable. That file is copied
verbatim into the deliverables tree rather than being reloaded and re-exported, because
a general-purpose mesh exporter does not round-trip glTF skinning data -- joint indices,
weights, skin and joint nodes, and inverse bind matrices -- and re-exporting would
silently strip the rig.

\subsection{Automatic Rigging}

The rigging stage fits a skeleton and skinning weights to a refined mesh. The mesh is
first centered on its bounding box and scaled to unit size, so that template proportions
are expressed relative to the model rather than in absolute units.

A template skeleton is then fitted to the mesh bounding box according to the requested
articulation type. The static template places a single root joint at the centroid; the
biped template places hips, spine, head, paired hands, and paired feet along the vertical
axis; the quadruped template places a body, neck, head, and four limb ends; and the winged
template places a body, head, paired wing tips, and a tail. Each template defines both the
joint hierarchy and the bones connecting them.

Skinning weights are then assigned by inverse distance. For a vertex $v_i$ and joint
$j$ at Euclidean distance $d_{ij}$, only the $k$ strongest influences are retained, and
the surviving weights are normalized to a partition of unity:
\begin{equation}
w_{ij}=\frac{1/d_{ij}}{\sum_{m \in \mathcal{K}_i} 1/d_{im}},\quad j \in \mathcal{K}_i
\label{eq}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{K}_i$ denotes the set of retained joints for vertex $i$ and $k =
|\mathcal{K}_i|$ is the configured influence limit. Distances in \eqref{eq} are clamped
away from zero so that a vertex coincident with a joint does not produce an unbounded
weight.

The weights are finally packed into the glTF skinning convention, in which each vertex
carries at most four joint indices and four corresponding weights; the retained
influences are re-normalized after packing so that each row still sums to one, and rows
with no positive influence are rejected rather than being silently bound to the first
joint. The stage emits a skinned GLB together with the skeleton, the weight matrix, and a
rig manifest, and its skinned output feeds the editing deliverable described above. The
lower band of Fig.~\ref{fig:geometry} shows this path and the point at which it rejoins
routing.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Functional Validation}

The current implementation demonstrates functional integration of the principal offline
workflow from heterogeneous input through reconstruction, refinement, and
application-specific output. A complete pipeline can execute ingestion, MASt3R
reconstruction, mesh refinement, and optional deliverable routing, as summarized
architecturally in Figs.~\ref{fig:overview}--\ref{fig:geometry}. Previously generated
ingestion schemas can also be replayed, enabling reconstruction experiments to be
separated from the original ingestion process.

\subsection{Reconstruction and Geometry Quality}

The refinement stage addresses several defects that can occur in raw learned
reconstruction. Component filtering removes isolated fragments, hole filling improves
surface completeness, Taubin smoothing reduces local irregularity, and normal
recomputation improves surface consistency. Vertex-color transfer allows the refined mesh
to retain the visual appearance of the reconstructed geometry. The frame-selection
strategy provides a practical mechanism for controlling computational cost: instead of
processing arbitrarily large video sequences, the pipeline limits reconstruction frames
and preferentially retains high-motion observations. Synchronized multi-camera inputs can
also be converted into cross-camera view groups using timestamp information.

\subsection{Computational Performance}

The implementation also reveals important computational constraints, summarized in
Table~\ref{tab1}. The project reports that refinement of a mesh containing approximately
2.6 million triangles can take around seven minutes even with smoothing disabled and
watertightness checking skipped; this figure is reported as a single implementation
observation rather than a controlled benchmark. The default smoothing configuration is
substantially slower. The refinement stage is consequently a major scalability bottleneck
and motivates GPU-based or more efficient mesh-processing approaches. TSDF fusion can
similarly become expensive for large reconstructions; the implemented fallback to dense
point-map export provides graceful degradation when fusion fails because of memory or
runtime constraints. This improves robustness but also means that the output
representation can depend on available computational resources.

\subsection{Robustness and Reproducibility}

Run manifests record the reconstruction model, execution device, pairing strategy, random
seed, and reconstruction configuration, supporting reproducible re-execution of a given
job. The TSDF-to-point-map fallback and the explicit non-watertight warning are further
robustness mechanisms that allow the pipeline to complete even when an individual stage
underperforms or fails.

\subsection{Discussion}

The current implementation should be regarded as systems-level validation rather than a
quantitative reconstruction benchmark. The project documentation does not report
standardized geometric metrics such as Chamfer distance, completeness, precision, recall,
or F-score, nor does it provide controlled comparisons against alternative reconstruction
systems. Accordingly, the results establish functional integration and practical behavior
but do not support claims of superior reconstruction accuracy.

\section{Conclusions}

This paper presented z-shift, a modular pipeline for converting heterogeneous
two-dimensional visual media into 3D representations. The system integrates media
classification and normalization, motion-aware sampling, multi-source synchronization,
MASt3R-based reconstruction, optional TSDF fusion, mesh refinement, and
application-specific delivery. The main contribution is the integration of these stages
into a reproducible workflow rather than a new reconstruction network. The architecture
explicitly separates ingestion, reconstruction, refinement, and delivery, enabling
individual components to be modified or evaluated independently. The implementation
demonstrates a functional offline path from heterogeneous input to refined GLB and
point-cloud assets. The results also expose practical limitations: large mesh refinement
remains computationally expensive, real-time delivery is incomplete, and quantitative
reconstruction evaluation has not yet been performed. Nevertheless, the system provides a
foundation for investigating the transition from isolated state-of-the-art reconstruction
algorithms toward complete 2D-to-3D asset-generation systems.

\section{Limitations}

The current system has several limitations. First, the reconstruction stage requires
multiple views and does not support single-image reconstruction. Second, RTSP and WebRTC
are not implemented, and although WebSocket live ingestion is available, the live outcome
pathway remains incomplete. Third, mesh refinement is CPU-bound and becomes expensive for
large reconstructions. Fourth, TSDF fusion can impose substantial memory and runtime
requirements. Fifth, the current viewing pathway exports a conventional colored point
cloud rather than a full Gaussian Splatting representation. Finally, auto-rigging fits
template skeletons to the mesh bounding box rather than predicting joints from geometry,
which assumes an axis-aligned, upright input, and the system lacks a formal quantitative
benchmark against competing reconstruction approaches. The current authentication,
rate-limiting, and streaming state are also in-memory and single-process, reflecting the
project's research-prototype status.

\section{Future Work}

Future work should first establish a quantitative evaluation framework using public
multi-view datasets and metrics such as Chamfer distance, point-to-surface error,
completeness, precision, recall, and F-score. Comparisons with classical MVS, DUSt3R, and
MASt3R pipelines would help isolate the benefits of the proposed orchestration and
preprocessing stages. A second priority is real-time reconstruction: the existing live
ingestion and backpressure mechanisms provide a starting point for WebRTC-based delivery,
incremental reconstruction, adaptive frame rates, and latency-aware processing. Third, GPU
acceleration and more efficient spatial algorithms should be investigated for mesh
refinement, and frame selection could be extended beyond motion scores by jointly
considering viewpoint diversity, image quality, feature richness, and reconstruction
uncertainty. Fourth, the viewing pathway could be extended toward 3D Gaussian Splatting
\cite{b8}, while the auto-rigging component could advance from template fitting toward
learned skeleton prediction to improve animation quality. Finally, future evaluation
should consider not only geometric accuracy but also downstream asset usability, rendering
performance, editing complexity, watertightness, and animation quality.
