\begin{abstract}
DUSt3R (Dense Unconstrained Stereo 3D Reconstruction) and MASt3R (Matching and
Stereo 3D Reconstruction) recover dense scene geometry from 2D observations
alone, without camera pose or prior calibration. A deployable 2D-to-3D system,
however, requires ingestion, frame selection, temporal synchronization,
geometry refinement, and application-specific delivery to work together. We
present z-shift, a four-stage pipeline in which each stage is replaceable, and
data passes between stages only as serializable artifacts. Ingestion unifies
image folders, videos, and synchronized multi-camera captures under a common
spatial schema, and buffers live WebSocket frames on a separate track.
Reconstruction uses MASt3R for the initial scene geometry, may
optionally fuse Truncated Signed Distance Functions (TSDFs), and falls back to
a point map when fusion is unavailable. Refinement applies a fixed sequence of
operations, while delivery routes GLB or point-cloud output under a
compatibility contract. Our evaluation combines a constrained benchmark suite,
one real capture traced through every stage at 8 and 24 views, and five DTU
scans scored against ground truth. Without preserving
capture order, motion-ranked frame retention degrades sliding-window pairing
locality 6.7-fold, while restoring order costs nothing. Refinement cost depends
more on disconnected mesh components than triangle count, remaining nearly
constant below approximately 100 components and rising close to linearly
thereafter, reaching 20-fold at 1,000 components; a batched labeling pass
removes that dependence. Repeating a run on the same
GPU and configuration produced byte-identical output on both GPUs tested.
Against DTU ground truth, ten views score 3.3--8.6~mm overall, and neither 49
views, restored capture order, nor refinement improved scores consistently. Object isolation removes most of a real mesh without lowering its score on four of five scans, and swapping in DUSt3R as a second reconstruction backend leaves every other stage unchanged.
Component- and integration-level correctness is established across the
pipeline. Reconstruction from a single image or a live stream, which the
pipeline rejects, real-time delivery, and efficient large-scale mesh processing
remain future work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The recovery of three-dimensional structure from two-dimensional imagery is a
well-known problem in computer vision and computer graphics. This topic has
progressed from recent approaches to image-based 3D reconstruction \cite{b2}
and Structure-from-Motion methods \cite{b3}, through deep learning-based 3D
reconstruction from multiple images \cite{b4} and learning-based multi-view
stereo \cite{b5}, to more recent approaches exploring pose-free dense
regression with DUSt3R \cite{b6} and MASt3R \cite{b7}, as well as learned
approaches such as MVSNeRF \cite{b13}, pixelNeRF \cite{b14}, Neural Radiance
Fields \cite{b16}, Instant-NGP \cite{b20}, 3D Gaussian Splatting \cite{b21},
and Mip-NeRF 360 \cite{b22}. While each of these techniques improves upon the
ability to perform three-dimensional reconstruction, an operational system
also requires input normalization, frame selection, synchronization, geometry
refinement, and output delivery tailored to the application.

z-shift is presented as a four-stage process where individually replaceable
stages exchange data in the form of serializable artifacts. The ingestion stage
unites image folders, videos, and synchronized captures, and buffers live
streams, under a uniform spatial representation; reconstruction accepts the
multi-view inputs and rejects single images and live streams. The reconstruction stage uses MASt3R optionally with
TSDF fusion and a fallback point-map representation, while the refinement and
delivery stages respectively handle geometry post-processing and routing of
outputs according to media type and application requirements. Thus, the focus
is on how heterogeneous media can move through the reconstruction and
refinement stages without tying the stages to any specific internal
representation.

The focus of our evaluation is on the performance of the stage interfaces. We
found that motion-ranked frame retention degrades sliding-window pairing by a
factor of 6.7 when frames are left unsorted, and that restoring capture order
recovers this at no additional cost. In contrast, refinement runtime is more
sensitive to mesh fragmentation than to the number of triangles, with the
transition occurring around 100 connected components and a 20x increase at
1,000 components in the studied configurations. Repeated runs on the same GPU
and configuration yielded byte-wise identical results. We then score the
assembled pipeline against DTU ground truth \cite{b19}, where restoring capture
order shows no reconstruction benefit and refinement lowers scores on real
scans, the opposite of its effect on our synthetic benchmark.

\section{Related Work}

Several established research directions in the field of 3D reconstruction
provide well-developed techniques for geometric reconstruction from multiple
images. Recent surveys of dense 3D reconstruction describe the geometric
foundations and datasets used for image-based reconstruction \cite{b2}, while
Herrera-Granda et al.\ \cite{b3} review Structure-from-Motion, visual odometry,
and SLAM methods for 3D reconstruction. Wang et al.\ \cite{b4} survey deep
learning-based approaches for 3D reconstruction from multiple images, while
Wang et al.\ \cite{b5} consider learning-based multi-view stereo for dense
reconstruction. Machine learning has enabled many contributions in the field of
3D reconstruction with neural representations like NeRF \cite{b16},
Instant-NGP \cite{b20}, 3D Gaussian Splatting \cite{b21}, and others
\cite{b13}, \cite{b14}, \cite{b22}. DUSt3R \cite{b6} and MASt3R \cite{b7}
enable dense geometry estimation with minimal reliance on conventional pose
estimation techniques. The above-mentioned contributions mostly deal with
reconstruction problems themselves, while the organization of other stages
depends on the application at hand.

In general, reconstructed geometry needs some processing to become useful as a
resource. Yang et al.\ \cite{b1} propose a feature-driven mesh denoising
technique aimed at reducing noise while preserving important geometric
features, while Maruani et al.\ \cite{b8} present a neural mesh representation
based on Quadric Error Metrics. Surface fusion of reconstructed geometry in
volumetric representations is shown by Hu et al.\ \cite{b9} through color
truncated signed distance field fusion, while Johari et al.\ \cite{b23}
reconstruct dense surface geometry using a hybrid representation of signed
distance fields. Kazhdan and Hoppe \cite{b24} present a distributed approach to
Poisson surface reconstruction for producing surface geometry from oriented
points. Even though the above-mentioned works address geometry processing
tasks, they use different representations of input geometry, assumptions, and
parameters.

\section{Research Gap and Problem Statement}

A stage may comply with its own specification yet violate an assumption made
by the following stage. It may also introduce a cost profile that does not
appear when stages are measured separately. This paper therefore focuses on
the correctness and cost of the interfaces between stages, rather than the
validity or cost of the model used in the reconstruction stage.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

Four modular stages make up the architecture: media ingestion, multi-view
reconstruction, mesh refinement, and outcome routing. They can communicate
only through serializable artifacts, namely a unified schema, a reconstruction
job, a mesh file, and a set of manifests. These four artifacts are the
system's interfaces. They define what one stage can assume about the previous
one, and they are where the behavior documented in
Section~\ref{sec:bench-validation} originates. Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} shows the
services and artifact stores that implement these boundaries.

\subsection{Media Ingestion}

An HTTP/WebSocket gateway with pluggable authentication and rate limiting
ingests single images, image folders, videos, video folders, and live
WebSocket frames. MIME type and file extension decide which processing track a
payload enters. Mixed or unrecognized payloads are rejected outright, never
partially processed. Normalization then follows on the batch track: images are
resized while aspect ratio and EXIF metadata are preserved, and videos are
probed using FFmpeg and sampled by a motion-adaptive sampler whose interval
shortens during motion and lengthens across static spans. When the input is a
video folder, a multi-source synchronizer estimates a per-source clock offset
by matching motion signatures against the source with the most frames, then
aligns frames to that anchor by nearest timestamp. On the live track, frames
are buffered instead in a bounded ring buffer with explicit backpressure.
Oversized payloads are denied, and a full buffer evicts its oldest frame.
Compute priority goes first to live work.

\subsection{Unified Spatial Representation}

Both tracks converge into a single serializable schema, which holds source
metadata, frame references, temporal information, per-frame intrinsics, and
synchronization state. Two benefits result at this boundary between ingestion
and reconstruction. Ingestion is decoupled from reconstruction, so that each
stage encodes as little as possible about the other's internal workings. A
normalized input also becomes replayable, which lets reconstruction
experiments run against a fixed frame set instead of the original capture.

\subsection{Multi-View Reconstruction}
\label{sec:reconstruction}

An explicit reconstruction job is created during the reconstruction stage. The
mode of operation depends on the type of input used: an image folder is
processed as multi-view, a single video as a video-sequence, and a folder of
videos as synchronized-views. Since alignment requires at least two views of
one subject, single images and live streams are rejected. For video jobs, the
system treats adjacent frames as potentially redundant and applies a
configurable frame budget to each job. Above that budget the highest-motion
frames are retained in preference to a uniform subsample. When processing
synchronized captures, the budget applies to entire groups rather than to
individual frames, so cross-camera groupings stay intact. Pairing then occurs
either through a complete scene graph or by sliding window, the latter
preferred for videos and larger image sets. On synchronized-views jobs,
cross-camera pairings form within each group, so that simultaneous
observations are matched directly. Frames then proceed to pairing in
motion-rank order, not capture order. Section~\ref{sec:bench-validation}
evaluates this cross-stage interaction.

Before alignment, the pipeline selects an execution device among CUDA, CPU,
and Metal backends, seeds the Python, NumPy, and PyTorch generators if a seed
has been supplied, and derives an approximate pinhole-intrinsic initialization
from the EXIF 35~mm-equivalent focal length \cite{b10}, assuming square
pixels, a centered principal point, and no lens distortion. The MASt3R backend
then performs sparse global alignment using its official implementation
\cite{b11}, with intermediate results cached to disk so that repeated runs
over the same frames reuse prior work. TSDF fusion may optionally precede
geometry export. If fusion is disabled by a user option, or fails under memory
or runtime pressure, the pipeline falls back to a dense point-map export and
records the event. GLB is the default output format for the colored mesh, with
OBJ and PLY also supported, and a confidence threshold discards low-confidence
points. A confidence-masked dense point cloud is written alongside it.
Recorded in the run manifest are the model, device, input resolution, pairing
strategy, thresholds, seed, and reproducibility metadata.

\subsection{Mesh Refinement}

To avoid operator and threshold selection on a per-model basis, a fixed
refinement procedure is applied to every mesh. The validation phase starts by
unwrapping container formats such as GLB, and rejects empty meshes and meshes
whose vertices have non-finite coordinates. The mode decides how components
are processed further. In object mode, only the largest connected component
remains. In room mode, the mesh is split into bodies, and components that meet
a minimum cell-count threshold are selected and merged.

Boundary holes are closed up to a size derived from the bounding-box diagonal,
but for slender geometry that is open along only one axis, hole closure is
skipped. Taubin's alternating low-pass filter is used to reduce surface noise
while avoiding the mesh shrinkage characteristic of Laplacian smoothing. In
room mode, feature edges above a configurable angle are preserved. The
finalization step merges vertices, triangulates, decimates towards the target
reduction, and recomputes consistent normals.

Smoothing and decimation change the vertex set, so vertex colors are mapped to
the new mesh by nearest-point transfer. Open boundary edges are counted to
check the watertightness of the mesh. A non-watertight result is reported to
the user as a warning rather than stopping pipeline execution.

\subsection{Application-Specific Deliverables}

Compatibility between source type and use case is treated as a contract at
the outcomes stage, rather than as a default behavior. Each use case declares
the source types it can meaningfully consume. Every other combination is
rejected with a routing error before anything is exported. All 24 combinations
route or reject correctly in our benchmark, as shown in Table~\ref{tab3}.

A Blender-ready GLB leaves through the editing pathway. The viewing pathway
packages the confidence-masked point cloud into a colored PLY, rather than
producing a full Gaussian Splatting representation \cite{b21}. Defined at the
routing level, the live pathway explicitly rejects requests.

\section{Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Computational Performance}
\label{sec:comp-performance}

Per-component processing overhead dominates refinement cost in the as-submitted
implementation, which splits the mesh into bodies on the CPU, while triangle
count has a smaller effect. Timings in this subsection are means over three
repeats (five for Table~\ref{tab-stage-profile}) on an Intel Core i7-14650HX
laptop (24 logical cores) under WSL2. When considered alone on a single
connected mesh, triangle count shows mild super-linear growth. A log-log fit
over 50K to 2.5M triangles gives an exponent of 1.10 to 1.21 across the eight
configuration and geometry combinations, with $R^2 \geq 0.98$ throughout.

At 2.5M triangles the default configuration (Taubin smoothing and the
watertightness check) adds 22--32\% over the smoothing-off baseline, and up to
50\% on meshes below 0.5M triangles, where fixed per-mesh costs weigh more.
The stronger driver is component count. At a fixed $\sim$96,000
triangles, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:refine-scaling}(a), cost grows 20x between 1
and 1,000 components on pooled means, and 17--24x across the four
configurations.

Nor is this growth a clean power law. The endpoint ratio alone would overstate
how evenly the cost accumulates. Fitted over the full component ladder, a
single log-log exponent of 0.38--0.43 (0.40 pooled) fits poorly, with
$R^2 = 0.77$, because the curve has a knee. Its local exponent is 0.15 from 1
to 100 components and 0.98 from 100 to 1,000.

Below roughly 100 components, fragmentation is nearly free, rising from
0.15~s to 0.31~s, about 2x over two decades. Above this point, cost rises
close to linearly with component count, supplying the remaining $\sim$9.6x.
Fragmentation therefore behaves as a threshold rather than a gradient.

Table~\ref{tab-stage-profile} attributes this cost to different substeps in
each regime. Finalization dominates on a large, connected mesh, at 55.8\%,
mostly point deduplication and normal recomputation. On a many-component mesh,
component filtering dominates instead, at 96.5\%, driven by repeated per-piece
processing during body splitting. A mechanical explanation for the knee
follows. Body splitting runs each connected component through a separate
processing pass, so once component count grows large enough for that loop to
dominate the fixed per-mesh work, cost tracks component count directly.

The explanation predicts its own fix, and we implemented it: one batched
connected-component labeling pass over the face adjacency graph, run on the
GPU, replaces the per-body loop and returns the same output (identical cell
and point counts on every scaling configuration and on the trace of
Section~\ref{sec:trace}). With it, cost no longer depends on component count
at all: 0.13~s at one component and 0.09~s at 1,000, and the fragmented
mesh of Table~\ref{tab-stage-profile} refines in 0.11~s instead of 3.07~s.
The slowest combined-load case falls from 34~s to 4.2~s.

This cost belongs to an interface, not to a stage. What sets refinement's
runtime is a structural property of the mesh that the reconstruction stage
happens to hand it, and no benchmark of refinement on a single connected mesh
would reveal it. The TSDF stage similarly has an explicit fallback to dense
point-map export when fusion exceeds available resources, at the price of an
output representation that depends on those resources.

\subsection{Determinism and Repeat-Run Behavior}
\label{sec:repeat-behavior}

Run manifests record the reconstruction model, device, pairing strategy, seed,
and configuration, which supports repeatable re-execution on the same
hardware. On three DTU scenes \cite{b19} (\texttt{scan24}, \texttt{scan37},
\texttt{scan40}), a small GPU benchmark evaluated this, running each twice
under one seed with the cache cleared between runs, with PyTorch's
deterministic-algorithms mode on and off. It was run on two machines: a Tesla~T4,
and an RTX~5060 laptop GPU (Blackwell, sm\_120) under WSL2 with CUDA~13 and
PyTorch~2.13, each on 8-view subsets of the scenes. On both, Hausdorff-95 and Chamfer
distance between repeats were exactly 0.0, point counts matched, and exported
GLB files were byte-identical by SHA-256, regardless of the deterministic
mode. That mode added $\sim$21\% wall time on the T4 and $\sim$39\% on the
RTX~5060 (mean 87.5\,s against 63.0\,s per run), without measurably improving
repeatability on either. PyTorch does not guarantee identical results across
releases, platforms, or CPU and GPU executions \cite{b29}, so reproducibility
in the general sense is not established by twelve runs on two devices. What
they show is narrower: the recorded manifest was sufficient for bit-identical
reruns on each tested environment, and enabling deterministic algorithms did
not improve repeatability in these trials. Identical output was not compared
\emph{across} the two machines.

\subsection{Component and Integration Correctness}
\label{sec:bench-validation}

Controlled validation of the individual components and their interfaces is
summarized in Table~\ref{tab3}, where every number traces to a committed CSV.
These are contract checks on a synthetic, single-body protocol. Component
filtering removes every injected fragment, and object and room mode converge
to the same output, as they should on a single body and would not on real
multi-body scenes. As injected corruption is removed, distance to the clean
ground truth improves monotonically (Fig.~\ref{fig:refine-quality}(a)--(b)),
which the injection protocol guarantees by construction. The informative
comparison there is between smoothing operators: Taubin holds mesh volume
within 0.16\% of the unsmoothed baseline, against 0.51\% drift for Laplacian
(Fig.~\ref{fig:refine-quality}(c)). Synchronization, motion-sampling, routing,
and color-transfer correctness are established by the remaining rows.

\subsubsection{Frame Ordering and Pairing Locality}
Motion-ranked retention above the frame budget
(Section~\ref{sec:reconstruction}) changes frame ordering before
sliding-window pairing, which assumes capture order. Re-sorting the retained
frames into capture order reduces the median pair-index gap from 186.3 to 27.7
across the three over-budget schedules. That is 6.7x pooled and 4.4x to 10.2x
per schedule, at no additional cost, and complete pairing is unaffected
because it does not depend on order. On its own, neither stage is incorrect:
selection returns the frames it was asked for, and pairing windows the
sequence it is given. Only when the two are put together does the defect
appear, and no test that exercises either stage alone would find it.
Section~\ref{sec:dtu} tests its effect on reconstruction and finds no benefit
from restoring order on DTU.

\subsection{Illustrative End-to-End Trace}
\label{sec:trace}

This subsection adds a traced run of the offline workflow in
Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} on a real CO3D \emph{teddybear} capture \cite{b15}
(sequence \texttt{187\_20215\_38541}), at two frame counts: eight evenly
spaced frames, the configuration of the submitted manuscript, and 24. We
include it to show the assembled pipeline running end to end at full scale
and to check whether the model in Section~\ref{sec:comp-performance} predicts
where the time goes. Each configuration is a single run, which supports
neither a timing claim nor a quality claim. Table~\ref{tab2} reports the
per-stage timings for this purpose only, on the RTX~5060 laptop GPU of
Section~\ref{sec:repeat-behavior}.

The eight-view run reproduces the submitted pipeline: complete pairing over 56
pairs, then refinement with per-body component filtering on the CPU. Of its
32~min, component filtering took 1,808~s, 99.97\% of refinement wall time,
while every other substep took under 0.3~s (Table~\ref{tab2}). The mesh has
1.84M triangles and is far past the component-count knee of
Section~\ref{sec:comp-performance}, where the benchmark predicts component
filtering will dominate the remaining stages. It does. Run on the same raw
mesh, batched labeling returns identical cell and point counts in 0.31~s.

Tripling the frame count exposed an interface defect that the benchmark
suite could not. Object mode keeps the largest connected component, on the
assumption that the subject is the largest thing reconstructed. On a real
capture the support surface is reconstructed too, as one sheet the subject
rests on, and it grows with the number of views. At eight views the kept
component was the floor with the bear attached; at 24 the floor was a
separate and larger piece, and the refined mesh (230K cells) was floor
alone. Every stage met its own contract throughout. The 24-view run in Table~\ref{tab2} therefore uses an
object-isolation step added to refinement: it removes the dominant plane
found by RANSAC when that plane holds at least 30\% of the faces (69\% here),
then keeps the largest remaining piece together with the fragments inside
its bounding box. The TSDF mesh breaks the subject into many fragments, and
the largest one held only 8\% of the cells once the floor was cut, so keeping
the largest piece alone would have kept part of the bear. The step is off by
default, which leaves the synthetic single-body benchmarks unchanged.

At 24 views alignment over 552 pairs took 588~s and peaked at 4.4~GB of GPU
memory, and the whole run took under 11~min, refinement included. Confidence
masking retained 41.5\% of points against 63.1\% at eight views. Both outputs
were correctly flagged as non-watertight, and the warning did not halt the
workflow: the pipeline reports geometry defects instead of terminating on
them.

\subsection{Reconstruction Accuracy on DTU}
\label{sec:dtu}

We score the pipeline against DTU's structured-light ground truth \cite{b19}
on five scans, using DTU's evaluation: accuracy over reconstructed points
inside the observation mask, completeness over ground-truth points above the
table plane, both ignoring distances over 20~mm, and overall error as their
mean. F-score uses $\tau = 2$~mm. All runs use 512-pixel input on the RTX~5060
laptop GPU of Section~\ref{sec:repeat-behavior}.

MVS methods are normally given DTU's cameras; MASt3R estimates its own. Each
reconstruction is therefore placed in DTU's frame by a similarity fit from its
estimated camera centers to the calibrated ones, the only use of DTU's
calibration in scoring. The fit is trimmed: a camera whose residual exceeds both
10~mm and three times the median is left out, and the fit repeated. A run is
flagged as a pose failure if the remaining RMSE exceeds 10~mm or more than 20\%
of its cameras are left out, since its scores then reflect pose error more than
surface error. We chose this rule after seeing single views up to 280~mm off in
these runs, so the results also record the untrimmed fit. Rigid ICP against the
ground truth, which a deployment could not run, is reported only to show how
much of the error is placement.

Table~\ref{tab:dtu} gives the results. Ten evenly spread views with complete
pairing (90 pairs) score 3.30--8.59~mm overall in 157~s on average. Forty-nine
views take 7.4x as long and do not reliably improve on this: overall error is
lower on two of the five scans and F-score higher only on scan24. Complete
pairing of 49 views (2,352 pairs) needs about 18~GB in the scene optimizer, so
these runs use a non-cyclic sliding window of 9 (792 pairs), and the comparison
changes the pairing graph along with the view count. A large share of the error
is placement rather than surface shape: at ten views, ICP lowers mean overall
error from 5.85~mm to 3.64~mm and raises mean F-score from 0.210 to 0.422.
Overall error can also flatter a failed run, since it ignores distant points:
scan37 at 49 views drops 37\% of its reconstructed points and 53\% of the ground
truth. Pose failures affect 3 of these 10 runs, including both on scan37.

Columns V1--V3 test the frame ordering of
Section~\ref{sec:bench-validation}. DTU's 49 views stand in for a video under a
40-frame budget and sliding-window pairing (240 pairs): V1 is motion-rank order
as implemented, V2 the same frames re-sorted into capture order, and V3 a uniform
subsample. Each runs under three seeds, but the seed reaches only TSDF sampling.
The camera fit is identical across seeds in every cell and overall error varies
by at most 0.044~mm (SD), so the sample is five scans rather than 15 runs.
Restoring capture order showed no benefit: V2 beats V1 on one scan of five, on
both overall error and F-score. Nor does this show that motion-rank order is
better. Depending on whether the fit is trimmed and ICP applied, V1 wins between
two and five of the five scans on overall error and between two and four on
F-score. Even five of five is only $p = 0.06$ on a two-sided sign test, the trim
was chosen post hoc, 10 of the 15 variant cells are pose failures, and DTU is a
camera rig with synthetic timestamps rather than a video. A sweep of the uniform
budget on scan24 (10, 20, 40, and all 49 frames, with a window of 3) gives 4.23,
3.90, 6.17, and 8.04~mm, so more frames did not help at that window; at all 49
frames, the wider window above reaches 4.28~mm.

Finally, we score raw and refined meshes at ten views, sampling the mesh surface
rather than the point cloud scored above, with object isolation off. On these
real scans refinement lowers scores. Object mode, which keeps 11--26\% of the
faces, changes mean F-score by $-0.020$ and mean overall error by $+0.65$~mm,
improving them on two and one of the five scans. Room mode, which keeps
79--86\%, changes them by $-0.021$ and $+0.44$~mm and improves neither on any
scan. Completeness worsens on every scan in object mode, and smoothing barely
matters (at most 0.05~mm between 0 and 15 iterations). The sign is the opposite
of the synthetic row of Table~\ref{tab:quality-repro}, where refinement removes
damage we injected; that row shows it removes such damage, not that it makes a
real reconstruction more accurate. Scan37 remains a pose failure here, and scan40
passes only at the limit, with 2 of its 10 cameras left out.

\subsection{Further Evaluation: Real Meshes, CO3D, Synchronization, and a Second Backend}
\label{sec:further}

Four further experiments use the same five DTU scans (24, 37, 40, 55, and 63)
and the CO3D capture of Section~\ref{sec:trace}. Their results are in
\texttt{bench/results/b10\_*} to \texttt{b13\_*}.

\emph{Refinement on real meshes.} We timed both component filters on the raw
meshes of the five scans (1.3--2.6M cells), with five repeats per
cell on the GPU path. Batched labeling takes 0.30--0.84~s per mesh (SD at most
0.09~s) and returns the same cell count as the per-body filter wherever the
latter finished. The per-body filter finished within the 30-min budget on one
scan, scan37, where it took 1,259~s, about 4,000 times longer. On the other four it did not finish. A
real mesh therefore lands far above the knee of
Section~\ref{sec:comp-performance}, as the CO3D mesh did, and the speedup
measured on synthetic meshes understates what the batched pass saves in
practice. Table~\ref{tab:isolation} shows what each object-mode variant keeps and
scores. Plain object mode keeps 11--26\% of the faces and lowers mean F-score
from 0.213 to 0.193. Object isolation, which cuts the dominant support plane
(32--60\% of the faces) before gathering the subject's fragments, keeps
15--43\% on four scans and matches or exceeds the raw mesh's F-score on those
four (mean 0.239 against 0.229 raw and 0.217 for object mode). On scan37,
which is a pose failure in every configuration of Table~\ref{tab:dtu}, the plane cut
removes the subject and the score collapses. Object isolation thus removes most
of the mesh without lowering the DTU score on four of five scans, while the
largest-component rule does not.

\emph{CO3D against its reference cloud.} CO3D's point cloud is a COLMAP
reconstruction rather than an independent ground truth, and the single-sequence
release has no camera annotations. We therefore register each result to it with
a fixed RMS-radius scale and a rigid ICP, report the registration residual
(1.4--1.5\% of the bounding-box diagonal), and treat the cloud as a
pseudo-reference. Accuracy and completeness are fractions of that diagonal.
At eight views the refined, isolated mesh reaches F-score 0.745 at 2\% of the
diagonal and accuracy 0.019, against 0.586 and 0.061 for the raw point cloud;
at 24 views the values are 0.769 and 0.016 against 0.502 and 0.058. Without
isolation the eight-view refined mesh reaches 0.603, so isolation accounts for
most of the gain there. Because registration to the reference favors any
well-shaped surface, these figures compare variants with one another and do not
rank the pipeline against other methods.

\emph{Synchronization on real images.} Section~\ref{sec:bench-validation}
validates the synchronizer on synthetic motion traces. Here we use the images
of the five DTU scans: the production sampler's motion score is computed on
consecutive real frames, a second camera's scores come from its own perturbed
pixels (gain, offset, blur, and sensor noise), and its clock is shifted by 0 to
640~ms with 0 or 20~ms of jitter, over two or three sources and five seeds
(1,100 trials). DTU's cameras carry no timestamps, so the clocks are
injected and recovery is scored against the injected offset. The median
absolute error is 12--30~ms per scan, against a 40~ms frame interval. The
recovered sign is correct in 82--95\% of trials with a nonzero offset, and
group formation is 83--90\%. The mean error (31--145~ms) is much larger than
the median because a minority of trials lock onto the wrong motion peak, which
the mean-error figure of the synthetic row does not show. This is a test of
the synchronizer on a real scene's signal, not on a multi-camera recording
with true clocks, which we did not collect.

\emph{A second reconstruction backend.} The reconstruction stage sits behind a
backend interface selected per job, and we ran DUSt3R \cite{b6} through it on
the five scans at the ten views of Table~\ref{tab:dtu}. DUSt3R has a different
network and a different global optimizer and performs no TSDF fusion. For
each of the five scans the stage wrote the same mesh, point cloud, camera, and
manifest artifacts, the manifest recorded the backend that ran, and refinement
consumed the mesh with no change to it or to delivery. A contract test states
what any backend must produce. The swap also changes the score, so the measurement is
sensitive to the stage and not to the interface: DUSt3R reaches a mean F-score of
0.069 and overall error of 8.3~mm, against 0.210 and 5.85~mm for MASt3R, with
all five scans flagged as pose failures, while taking 64~s per scan against
157~s. This shows that the reconstruction stage can be replaced and its effect
measured under one protocol; we make no claim of this kind for the other stages.


\section{Conclusions}

This paper presents a system architecture of independently replaceable stages
communicating through explicit artifacts (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}), and not a
new reconstruction network. Its findings concern how those stages fit
together. Correct at the selection stage, frame ordering nonetheless breaks
pairing locality at the next one, and restoring capture order recovers a 6.7x
pair-index gap for free. Mesh fragmentation, a structural property of what
reconstruction hands refinement, governs refinement cost in the as-submitted
implementation far more than triangle count does, and it does so as a
threshold rather than a gradient: cost is nearly flat below roughly 100
components, then close to linear in component count, reaching 20x at 1,000.
That shape, more than the endpoint ratio, is what makes cost predictable in
advance, since a single structural property of the incoming mesh determines
which regime a run lands in. It also pointed at the fix: a batched labeling
pass makes refinement cost independent of component count. Repeat runs of a fixed configuration on each of two GPUs
were deterministic down to the byte, with identical SHA-256 mesh hashes.

None of this would surface from a benchmark of a single stage on its own.
Component correctness does not imply system correctness or predictable system
cost. Nor does a synthetic gain imply a real one: against DTU ground truth,
the pipeline scores 3.3--8.6~mm overall at ten views, much of it placement
error from estimated cameras, and restoring capture order, adding views, and
refinement each failed to improve scores consistently.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

Three principal limitations attach to the prototype. Real-time delivery is
incomplete: WebSocket ingestion works, but RTSP/WebRTC and the live output
route are unimplemented, and viewing exports a colored point cloud instead of
Gaussian Splatting. Batched labeling removed the fragmentation cost, but the
rest of refinement stays on the CPU, where finalization dominates large
connected meshes. The ground-truth benchmark covers five scans of one dataset at
512-pixel input, camera pose fails on 3 of its 10 view-count runs and 10 of its
15 frame-ordering cells, and no video with ground truth was tested.
Reconstruction requires at least two views, and authentication, rate limiting,
and streaming state remain in-memory and single-process, reflecting
research-prototype status.

Our DTU scores characterize this pipeline and, at ten views, its DUSt3R variant; published MVS scores, which are given DTU's cameras, are not
comparable. More robust camera estimation, and comparison against classical MVS
baselines under one protocol using Chamfer distance,
point-to-surface error, and F-score, are the priorities for future work.
