\begin{abstract}
Recent advances in computer vision have substantially improved 3D reconstruction
from two-dimensional observations, but a deployable 2D-to-3D system must also
compose that reconstruction with ingestion, frame selection, temporal
synchronization, geometric refinement, and application-specific delivery. This
paper presents z-shift, a systems contribution rather than a new reconstruction
network: a fixed sequence of independently replaceable stages -- ingestion of
images, videos, synchronized captures, and live frames through a unified spatial
schema; MASt3R-based multi-view reconstruction with optional TSDF fusion and a
point-map fallback; automated mesh refinement; and contract-checked routing of
GLB or point-cloud deliverables -- communicating only through explicit,
serializable artifacts. A controlled benchmark suite, with a single traced
eight-view capture as an end-to-end illustration, shows
that the interfaces between stages, not the stages themselves, carry the
system's most consequential behavior. Motion-ranked frame retention degrades
sliding-window pairing locality unless capture order is restored, a 6.6x effect
fixable at zero cost; refinement cost is governed by mesh fragmentation rather
than triangle count, and behaves as a threshold -- nearly flat below roughly 100
components, then close to linear, reaching 23x at 1,000; and repeat runs of the
same configuration on one GPU were byte-identical. Component- and
integration-level correctness is established across the pipeline; real-time
delivery, efficient large-scale mesh processing, and quantitative reconstruction
accuracy against ground truth remain future work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recovering three-dimensional structure from two-dimensional images is a fundamental
problem in computer vision and graphics. Classical structure-from-motion (SfM) and
multi-view stereo (MVS) methods estimate camera geometry, correspondences, depth, and
surfaces from multiple observations \cite{b1}--\cite{b4}, but are sensitive to
viewpoint coverage, image quality, and correspondence reliability. Learning-based
methods have since expanded these capabilities: Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)
introduced a continuous neural scene representation for view synthesis \cite{b6},
with follow-on work on faster rendering \cite{b7}, explicit representations
\cite{b8}, large-scale \cite{b9}, and generalized reconstruction \cite{b10},
\cite{b11}. DUSt3R introduced dense 3D reconstruction from image pairs without a
conventional calibrated pipeline \cite{b14}, and MASt3R strengthened this through
improved image matching \cite{b15}.

This paper addresses the question that remains once a capable reconstruction
engine is available: how the surrounding stages -- ingestion, frame selection,
synchronization, refinement, and delivery -- are composed around it so that
heterogeneous raw media reach a usable deliverable at a predictable cost,
reproducibly. z-shift is that composition: a fixed sequence of
independently replaceable stages communicating only through explicit, serializable
artifacts, with MASt3R occupying the reconstruction slot. The evaluation
supports one observation: individually correct stages do not compose into a
correct or predictable system for free -- the interfaces between them carry
their own correctness and cost behavior.

Real-world input is heterogeneous -- photographs, image directories, videos,
synchronized multi-camera captures, continuous frame streams -- and video adds
redundancy and cost, so z-shift normalizes these sources into a common spatial
schema and bounds the frames entering reconstruction
(Section~\ref{sec:architecture}). Raw reconstruction is in turn not
production-ready: surfaces may contain disconnected
components, holes, noise, and inconsistent normals, which established
mesh-processing methods can address \cite{b16}--\cite{b19}; z-shift applies these
automatically in a dedicated refinement stage that preserves vertex colors.

This work contributes, as systems abstractions rather than new algorithms: (1) a
unified ingestion schema decoupling heterogeneous batch and live media from
everything downstream and making a normalized input replayable; (2) bounded
orchestration of frame selection and pairing under an explicit frame budget; (3) a
backend orchestration layer wrapping MASt3R with optional TSDF fusion and a
recorded point-map fallback; (4) an automated geometry-processing stage applying a
fixed refinement sequence without per-model human choices; and (5) a delivery
layer enforcing source-type/use-case compatibility as an explicit contract and
emitting reproducibility manifests. It further contributes three empirical
findings about how these stages interact: frame ordering degrades pairing
locality (6.6x, fixable at zero cost); mesh fragmentation rather than triangle
count governs refinement cost, with a threshold near 100 components rather than
a smooth gradient; and repeat runs of a fixed configuration on one GPU are
byte-identical, so a run is reproducible from its manifest where hardware is
held fixed.

\section{Related Work}

Classical SfM and MVS recover camera poses and dense geometry from
correspondences across images \cite{b1}--\cite{b4} but degrade on textureless,
specular, or sparsely covered surfaces. Learned scene representations relax that
dependence -- NeRF and its accelerated, explicit, and unbounded-scene successors
\cite{b6}--\cite{b9}, and generalizable one- or few-view variants \cite{b10},
\cite{b11} -- but optimize for view synthesis rather than an explicit, exportable
surface, limiting their direct use as an asset-production backend.

DUSt3R \cite{b14} and MASt3R \cite{b15} are the most directly relevant prior
work: both regress dense per-pixel point maps in a common reference frame from
unposed image pairs, removing calibration and pose as prerequisites, and MASt3R
adds a dense local-feature head that improves matching. Both yield a
confidence-weighted point map rather than a clean surface, motivating the
refinement stage described later. z-shift adopts MASt3R as its current backend;
because stages communicate only through serializable artifacts
(Section~\ref{sec:architecture}), it could be swapped for a comparable method
without changing the surrounding stages. The volumetric fusion
\cite{b21}--\cite{b23} and mesh-processing \cite{b16}--\cite{b19} methods z-shift
composes are likewise established. The contribution is the composed system and
what can be said about it once composed: which interface properties determine
its cost, and what a run must record to be reproducible.

z-shift therefore belongs to a smaller literature of complete reconstruction
\emph{systems} rather than individual algorithms. COLMAP chains incremental SfM
\cite{b2} with dense multi-view stereo \cite{b25}, and AliceVision's Meshroom
\cite{b26} wraps a similar chain in a node-based GUI for interactive,
human-supervised use; Nerfstudio \cite{b27} standardizes data
loading, training, and export for radiance-field methods; and Open3D \cite{b28}
and Kaolin \cite{b29} supply reusable primitives without prescribing an
ingestion-to-delivery workflow. z-shift differs from the classical pipelines in
adopting an unposed learned backend in place of calibrated SfM/MVS, and from all
four in targeting a fixed, non-interactive path from heterogeneous raw media
through automatic refinement to use-case-routed export, rather than an
interactive authoring tool or a library the user must assemble. Two consequences follow.
Because mesh quality is conventionally restored by a human choosing operators and
thresholds per model \cite{b18}, \cite{b19}, an automatic pipeline must fix the
sequence in advance, which makes its cost a property of the input's structure
rather than of an operator's choices. And because reconstruction accuracy is
conventionally reported against captured ground truth on datasets such as
ScanNet \cite{b34}, such protocols evaluate a reconstruction method in isolation
and say nothing about whether the surrounding stages preserve the properties a
downstream tool requires -- the gap the benchmark reported later addresses.

\section{Research Gap and Problem Statement}

The reconstruction components surveyed above are individually well studied and,
in isolation, work. Deploying them additionally requires ingestion, frame
selection, synchronization, refinement, and delivery, and those stages must
compose. Composition is a distinct problem: a stage that satisfies its own
specification can still violate an assumption the next stage makes, or exhibit a
cost profile that no stage shows alone. This work addresses that gap -- the
correctness and cost of the interfaces between stages -- as distinct from the
accuracy of whichever model occupies the reconstruction slot.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

The architecture is four modular stages -- media ingestion, multi-view
reconstruction, mesh refinement, and outcome routing -- communicating only through
explicit, serializable artifacts: a unified schema, a reconstruction job, a mesh
file, and a set of manifests. Those four artifacts are the system's interfaces:
each is what one stage may assume about the previous one, and each is where the
composition-level behavior reported in Section~\ref{sec:bench-validation}
originates. Fig.~\ref{fig:overview} summarizes the flow.

\subsection{Media Ingestion}

Ingestion accepts single images, image folders, videos, video folders, and live
WebSocket frames through an HTTP/WebSocket gateway with pluggable authentication
and rate limiting. A classifier selects a processing track from MIME type and file
extension, rejecting mixed or unrecognized payloads outright rather than
partially processing them. The batch track normalizes uploads: images are resized
with aspect ratio and EXIF metadata preserved, while videos are probed with FFmpeg
and sampled by a motion-adaptive sampler that shortens its interval during motion
and lengthens it across static spans. For video folders, a multi-source
synchronizer estimates a per-source clock offset by matching motion signatures
against the source with the most frames, then aligns frames to that anchor by
nearest timestamp. The live track instead buffers frames in a bounded ring buffer
with explicit backpressure -- oversized payloads are refused, a full buffer evicts
its oldest frame -- and receives top compute priority.

\subsection{Unified Spatial Representation}

Both tracks converge into a single serializable schema holding source metadata,
frame references, temporal information, per-frame intrinsics, and synchronization
state. This is the pipeline's first explicit artifact boundary, and it buys two
things. It decouples ingestion from reconstruction, so neither stage encodes
assumptions about the other's internals; and it makes a normalized input
replayable, so reconstruction experiments run against a fixed frame set rather
than against the original capture.

\subsection{Multi-View Reconstruction}
\label{sec:reconstruction}

The reconstruction stage converts the schema into an explicit reconstruction job.
Each source type maps to a mode -- image folder to multi-view, single video to
video-sequence, video folder to synchronized-views -- while single images and live
streams are rejected, since alignment requires at least two views of one subject.
Video is redundant, so the job is capped at a configurable frame budget: above it,
the highest-motion frames are retained in preference to a uniform subsample, and
for synchronized captures the budget applies to whole groups so cross-camera
groups are never split. Pairing then uses either a complete scene graph or a
sliding window, the latter preferred for videos and larger image sets; for
synchronized-views jobs, cross-camera pairs are formed within each group so
simultaneous observations are matched directly. Retained frames reach pairing in
motion-rank order rather than capture order -- a cross-stage interaction
Section~\ref{sec:bench-validation} measures.

The pipeline resolves an execution device
across CUDA, CPU, and Metal backends, seeds the Python, NumPy, and PyTorch generators
when a seed is supplied, and converts an EXIF 35~mm-equivalent focal length into the
per-image intrinsic matrix supplied to alignment. MASt3R then performs sparse global
alignment, caching intermediate results on disk so repeated runs over the same
frames reuse prior work. Geometry export is optionally preceded by TSDF fusion;
when fusion is disabled, or fails under memory or runtime pressure, the pipeline
falls back to a dense point-map export and records the fallback. The exporter
writes a colored mesh -- GLB by default, with OBJ and PLY also supported --
applying a confidence threshold to discard low-confidence points, and
additionally writes a confidence-masked dense point cloud; a run manifest records
the model, device, input resolution, pairing strategy, thresholds, seed, and
reproducibility metadata.

\subsection{Mesh Refinement}

The refinement stage applies a fixed operator sequence, rather than the per-model
operator and threshold choices a human would make in an interactive tool. It first
validates its input, unwrapping container formats such as GLB scenes and rejecting
meshes that are empty or contain non-finite coordinates. Component handling then
depends on mode: object mode retains only the largest connected component, while
room mode splits the mesh into bodies, keeps every component above a minimum cell
count, and merges the survivors, preserving scenes legitimately composed of several
pieces. Boundary holes are closed up to a maximum size derived from the
bounding-box diagonal, skipping closure when the geometry is itself thin and open
along one axis so an intentionally planar capture is not sealed.

Surfaces are smoothed with Taubin smoothing, which suppresses noise without the
shrinkage of repeated Laplacian passes; room mode additionally preserves feature
edges above a configurable angle. Finalization merges coincident points,
triangulates, optionally decimates to a target reduction, and recomputes
outward-facing normals. Because smoothing and decimation rewrite the vertex set,
vertex colors are carried onto the cleaned mesh by nearest-point transfer, so
appearance survives the geometric operations -- an invariant the interface between
these two stages must hold and that Section~\ref{sec:bench-validation} checks. A
final pass counts open boundary edges and flags the mesh watertight or not,
reporting a non-watertight result as a warning rather than terminating the
workflow, alongside a refinement manifest recording input and output counts, the
watertightness result, and any warnings.

\subsection{Application-Specific Deliverables}

The outcomes stage treats source-type/use-case compatibility as an explicit
contract rather than a default: each use case declares the source types it can
meaningfully consume, every other combination is rejected with a routing error
before anything is exported, and the benchmark (Table~\ref{tab3}) confirms all 24
combinations route or reject correctly. The editing pathway exports a
Blender-ready GLB; the viewing pathway packages the confidence-masked point cloud
into a colored PLY rather than a full Gaussian Splatting representation; the live
pathway is defined at the routing level and rejects requests explicitly.

In the current implementation, refinement cost is dominated by per-component
processing overhead rather than by triangle count. On a single connected mesh,
triangle count alone is only mildly super-linear: a log-log fit over 50K--2.5M
triangles gives an exponent of 1.16--1.31 across the eight
configuration/geometry combinations ($R^2 > 0.98$ throughout), and the default
configuration adds only 23\% over the smoothing-off baseline. Component count,
held at a fixed $\sim$96,000 triangles, is the stronger driver
(Fig.~\ref{fig:refine-scaling}(a)): cost grows 23x between 1 and 1,000
components on the pooled means, 17--33x across the four configurations.

That growth is not a clean power law, and the endpoint ratio alone would
overstate how evenly it accumulates. A single log-log fit over the component
ladder gives an exponent of 0.39--0.48 (0.43 pooled) but fits poorly
($R^2 = 0.85$), because the curve has a knee: the local exponent is 0.22 from 1
to 100 components and 0.94 from 100 to 1,000. Below roughly 100 components
fragmentation is nearly free -- 0.45~s to 1.23~s, under 3x over two decades --
and above it cost rises close to linearly in component count, supplying the
remaining $\sim$8.5x. Fragmentation is therefore a threshold rather than a
gradient: refinement stays cheap until component count reaches the low hundreds,
past which each additional component costs roughly a fixed amount. The two axes
are also super-additive, the combined configuration costing roughly 4x the
additive prediction and reaching the $\sim$7-minute regime marked in
Fig.~\ref{fig:refine-scaling}(b).

Table~\ref{tab-stage-profile} attributes this cost to different substeps in each
regime: on a large connected mesh, finalization dominates (53.7\%, mostly point
deduplication and normal recomputation); on a many-component mesh, component
filtering dominates instead (95.9\%), driven by repeated per-piece processing
during body splitting. The knee above therefore has a mechanical explanation:
body splitting runs each connected component through a separate processing pass,
so once component count is large enough for that loop to dominate the fixed
per-mesh work, cost tracks component count directly. A batched
connected-component labeling pass is the direct optimization and was not
implemented for this study. The cost is a property of an interface rather than
of a stage: refinement's runtime is set by a structural property of the mesh the
reconstruction stage happens to hand it, which no benchmark of refinement on a
single connected mesh would reveal. TSDF fusion can similarly become expensive;
the fallback to dense point-map export degrades gracefully, at the price of an
output representation that depends on available resources.

\subsection{Determinism and Repeat-Run Behavior}

Run manifests record the reconstruction model, device, pairing strategy, seed,
and configuration, supporting repeatable re-execution on the same hardware. A
small GPU benchmark tested this on three scenes (\texttt{scan24},
\texttt{scan37}, \texttt{scan40}), each run twice on one Tesla~T4 under one seed
with the cache cleared between runs. Hausdorff-95 and Chamfer distance between
repeats were exactly 0.0, point counts matched, and exported GLB files were
byte-identical (SHA-256), regardless of PyTorch's deterministic-algorithms mode
-- which added $\sim$21\% wall time without measurably improving repeatability
here. Six runs on one device do not establish reproducibility in the general
sense; they show that the manifest captures enough state to reproduce a run
bit-for-bit where the hardware is held fixed, and that the deterministic-kernel
setting is not what buys it.

\subsection{Component and Integration Correctness}
\label{sec:bench-validation}

Table~\ref{tab3} summarizes controlled validation of the individual components
and their interfaces; every number traces to a committed CSV. These are contract
checks on a synthetic, single-body protocol: component filtering removes every
injected fragment, and object and room mode converge to the same output, as they
should on a single body and would not on real multi-body scenes. Distance to the
clean ground truth improves monotonically as injected corruption is removed
(Fig.~\ref{fig:refine-quality}(a)--(b)), which the injection protocol largely
guarantees; the informative comparison there is between smoothing operators,
where Taubin holds mesh volume within 0.15\% of the unsmoothed baseline against
0.52\% drift for Laplacian (Fig.~\ref{fig:refine-quality}(c)). The remaining
rows establish synchronization, motion-sampling, routing, and color-transfer
correctness.

\textbf{Frame ordering and pairing locality.} Motion-ranked retention above the
frame budget (Section~\ref{sec:reconstruction}) changes frame ordering before
sliding-window pairing, which assumes capture order. Re-sorting the retained
frames into capture order reduces the median pair-index gap from 184.5 to 27.8
across the three over-budget schedules -- 6.6x pooled, 4.4x to 10x per schedule
-- at zero additional cost, and leaves complete pairing, which does not depend on
order, unchanged. Neither stage is incorrect in isolation: selection returns the
frames it was asked for, and pairing windows the sequence it is given. The defect
exists only in their composition, and is invisible to any test that exercises
either stage alone. Its effect on reconstruction quality is not measured
here.

Together these establish component-level correctness against synthetic ground
truth, integration correctness across the routing, manifest, and color-transfer
invariants, and systems-level behavior under controlled conditions -- scaling,
determinism, and robustness to injected corruption. End-to-end reconstruction
accuracy against captured ground truth is a separate question, addressed by the
protocols in Section~\ref{sec:future-work} rather than here.

\section{Conclusions}

z-shift is a systems architecture of independently replaceable stages
communicating through explicit artifacts (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}), not a new
reconstruction network. Its findings are accordingly about composition. Frame
ordering, correct at the selection stage, breaks pairing locality at the next
one, and restoring capture order recovers a 6.6x pair-index gap at zero cost.
Mesh fragmentation, a structural property of what reconstruction hands
refinement, governs refinement cost in this implementation far more than triangle
count does, and it does so as a threshold: cost is nearly flat below roughly 100
components and then close to linear in component count, reaching 23x at 1,000.
That shape, not the endpoint ratio, is what makes cost predictable in advance: a
single structural property of the incoming mesh determines which regime a run
lands in. Repeat runs of a fixed
configuration on one GPU were deterministic down to the byte (identical SHA-256
mesh hashes). None of these is visible from a benchmark of any single stage in
isolation, which is the general point: component correctness does not imply
system correctness or predictable system cost. What remains outside this
evaluation is end-to-end reconstruction accuracy against captured ground truth
(Section~\ref{sec:future-work}).

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

The prototype has three principal limitations. Real-time delivery is incomplete:
WebSocket ingestion works, but RTSP/WebRTC and the live outcome route are
unimplemented, and viewing exports a colored point cloud rather than Gaussian
Splatting. Refinement is CPU-bound and expensive on fragmented meshes. And there
is no end-to-end ground-truth reconstruction benchmark. Reconstruction also
requires at least two views, and authentication, rate limiting, and streaming
state remain in-memory and single-process, reflecting research-prototype status.

The evaluation therefore establishes component-, integration-, and systems-level
behavior, not reconstruction accuracy; quantitative comparison -- Chamfer
distance, point-to-surface error, and F-score against classical MVS, DUSt3R, and
MASt3R baselines -- remains the priority for future work.
